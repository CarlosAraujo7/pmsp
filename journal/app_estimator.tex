\section{The cross-fitted headroom estimator}
\label{app:estimator}
\paragraph{Setting} Fix an instance $x$ and write $\mu_a=\mu_a(x)$. One run of action $a$ returns the relative makespan $Y_a=\mu_a+\varepsilon_a$, with independent, mean-zero noise of variance $\sigma^2$ across runs and actions (the argument needs only independence and finite variance; normal noise is used in the simulation). The random control has an expected makespan $\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}$, a constant for all estimators below (in the experiments it is estimated on runs independent of those used to choose actions; in the simulation $\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}=\frac1A\sum_a\mu_a$, a uniformly random fixed action). The \emph{headroom} is $H=\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}-\min_a\mu_a$. The two bounds below hold for any constant $\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}$, whatever its sign.

\paragraph{One-seed oracle} $\hat H_1=\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}-\min_aY_a$. Because the minimum is a concave function, Jensen's inequality gives $\mathbb E[\min_aY_a]\le\min_a\mathbb E[Y_a]=\min_a\mu_a$, hence $\mathbb E[\hat H_1]\ge H$. With all $\mu_a$ equal and normal noise, $\mathbb E[\hat H_1]=\sigma\,\mathbb E[\max_{a\le A}Z_a]$ for independent standard normals $Z_a$, which grows like $\sigma\sqrt{2\ln A}$: the estimator reports a headroom that increases with the noise and with the number of actions even when there is none.

\paragraph{Cross-fitted oracle} Split the runs into two independent batches of sizes $k_1$ and $k_2$. Choose $\hat a=\arg\min_a\bar Y_a^{(1)}$ on the first batch and report $\hat H_{\mathrm{cf}}=\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}-\bar Y_{\hat a}^{(2)}$ from the second. Conditional on the first batch, $\hat a$ is fixed and the second batch is independent of it, so $\mathbb E[\bar Y_{\hat a}^{(2)}\mid\hat a]=\mu_{\hat a}$ and
\[
\mathbb E[\hat H_{\mathrm{cf}}]=\mu_{\mathrm{rand}}-\mathbb E[\mu_{\hat a}]=H-\underbrace{\mathbb E[\mu_{\hat a}-\min_a\mu_a]}_{\text{regret of the selection}}\le H .
\]
The estimator is therefore unbiased for the gain of the \emph{selected} action and a lower bound on the headroom; its bias is the expected regret, which is zero when all actions are equal or the selection is perfect and which falls as $k_1$ grows. Together with the one-seed bound this gives Proposition~\ref{prop:bounds}: $\mathbb E[\hat H_{\mathrm{cf}}]\le H\le\mathbb E[\hat H_1]$.

\paragraph{Why not simply average more runs?} Choosing and evaluating on the \emph{same} $k$ runs, $\mathbb E[\min_a\bar Y_a]\le\min_a\mu_a$ for every $k$, so the upward bias shrinks with $k$ (like $\sigma/\sqrt k$ when the actions are equal) but does not vanish at a finite budget. In the simulation of Table~\ref{tab:sim}, with $15$ actions, $\sigma=1$ and no true headroom, the best of four runs on the same runs still reports $0.87$ points. Cross-fitting spends the same runs but removes the selection bias at the price of a downward bias of known sign. For reporting, the bracket $[\hat H_{\mathrm{cf}},\hat H_1]$ is what matters; the rule of use of the paper is stated for the lower end.

\paragraph{Simulation} Table~\ref{tab:sim} confirms the two inequalities and the zero-headroom case in $18$ configurations ($A\in\{5,15\}$, $\tau\in\{0,0.25,1\}$, $\sigma\in\{0.5,1,2\}$, $20{,}000$ instances each). $\mathbb E[\hat H_1]$ lies above $H$ in every row and, with $\tau=0$, equals $\sigma\,\mathbb E[\max Z]$ up to simulation error ($0.58$, $1.16$ and $2.32$ for $A=5$ and $\sigma=0.5,1,2$); averaged over the $18$ configurations its bias is $+1.31$ points. $\hat H_{\mathrm{cf}}$ lies below $H$ by the regret of the selection (the two differ by less than $0.025$ in every row, which is simulation error), is within $0.01$ of zero when $\tau=0$, and has a mean bias of $-0.19$ points. The downward bias grows with the noise: with five actions and $\tau=1$ the regret is $0.07$, $0.21$ and $0.48$ points for $\sigma=0.5,1,2$. When the noise is large relative to the spread of the action effects, no estimator identifies the headroom well, and the bracket is wide.

\paragraph{Real data} Table~\ref{tab:empirical} repeats the comparison on the unrelated-machine battery, where four runs per action and instance are available. The one-seed oracle ($1.50$ and $2.81$ points) falls as more runs are used to choose and evaluate on the same runs ($1.32$ and $2.63$ with two, $1.19$ and $2.46$ with four), and the cross-fitted value ($0.43$ and $1.60$) lies below all of them, as the theory predicts. The gap between the best of four on the same runs and the cross-fitted value is the part of the oracle that selection on the evaluation runs inflates.
