\begin{abstract}
Learning-guided metaheuristics are routinely reported to beat non-learning counterparts, yet comparisons seldom separate what the learned policy adds from what the search structure, the CPU budget and the tuning already provide.
We propose a budget-fair attribution protocol (random control, equal CPU time, a static competitor validated offline, a cross-fitted oracle headroom, a power check, pre-recorded hypotheses) and apply it to Iterated Greedy (IG) for identical parallel machines (synthetic classes and more than $13{,}000$ published instances), with a flowshop, a genetic algorithm and unrelated machines as checks.
Three findings. The destruction--reconstruction mechanism, not the policy, explains the IG's advantage, and the share of a local search's gain that is ``only budget'' ranges from $28\%$ to $93\%$ with the implementation.
On the synthetic classes no online learner beats random choice at equal iterations by more than $0.04$ points, those that win at equal CPU time do so by choosing cheaper actions, and on unrelated machines, where the headroom is $12$--$23$ times larger, the best learner gets at most $18\%$ of what a validated action gains; a static configuration (validated offline, or a validated mixture when actions are complementary) is never worse than the online learner beyond the equivalence margin.
The oracle headroom net of run-to-run noise is a necessary condition for Q-Learning to pay, not a sufficient one (post hoc, outside the cells that set the threshold: Q-Learning gains beyond the margin in none of $71$ cells below about $0.05$ points and in $29$ of the $79$ above it), and a selector trained offline captures most of that headroom through class-level knowledge, whereas tuning, reward shaping, pre-training and optimistic initialization do not rescue the online learner.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Metaheuristics that learn while they search are a prominent line of work in evolutionary computation and operations research. A typical \emph{learnheuristic}~\cite{CalvetEtal2017} embeds a
reinforcement-learning agent, most often tabular Q-Learning~\cite{WatkinsDayan1992,SuttonBarto2018}, as an adaptive selector of operators, strategies or parameters inside a search framework such as
Iterated Local Search (ILS)~\cite{LourencaoEtal2019} or Iterated Greedy (IG)~\cite{RuizStutzle2007}, and reports that it beats the search without the agent or published baselines~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023,MissaouiOSullivan2026}.
Such results are usually read as evidence that the \emph{learned policy} is what helps.

Three confounds make that reading fragile. First, the \emph{search structure} that hosts the agent (which operators, which acceptance rule, whether there is a local search) may produce most of the gain
under any selection rule. Second, the \emph{budget}: an agent changes the CPU time per iteration and a local search multiplies it, so equal iteration counts are not equal budgets. Third, the \emph{tuning}:
a parameter validated offline on separate instances is a competitor that most studies omit. Adaptive operator selection is a well-studied topic~\cite{FialhoEtal2010,PisingerRopke2007}, but we found it rarely
answered with these controls: in an audit of $23$ recent open-access papers that embed a learned selector in a metaheuristic, $10$ include a random-selection control, $6$ compare at a common time limit,
$3$ include a static configuration tuned offline and $22$ report a benefit of the learned component ($5$ of them only partly) (a small sample, one coder; details in the repository).

We ask three questions. \textbf{RQ1:} what explains the advantage of a learning-guided IG over ILS, and how much of a local search's gain is only budget? \textbf{RQ2:} do online learners beat random choice over the same actions, at equal iterations and at equal CPU time, does a static configuration win anyway, and could learning have shown up if it existed?
\textbf{RQ3:} where, if anywhere, does an online selector pay, can that be foretold, and what exploits the headroom instead? Our contributions are
(i) a \emph{budget-fair attribution protocol} (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) with a cross-fitted oracle headroom, a power check, a positive control and a split of a learner's gain at equal CPU time into a decision effect and a cost effect, released as a small library;
(ii) evidence that the advantage of the learning-guided IG comes from the search structure, that the share of a local search's gain that is ``only budget'' depends on the efficiency of the implementation, and that a learner wins at equal CPU time mainly by choosing cheaper actions; and
(iii) a \emph{headroom analysis}: the usual headroom estimate is mostly run-to-run noise, and with the noise removed the headroom is a necessary condition for Q-Learning to pay, though not a sufficient one; a policy learned \emph{offline} exploits it, and four repairs of the online learner do not.
Section~\ref{sec:flowshop} checks how far the findings carry on a flowshop, a genetic algorithm and unrelated machines (real headroom, threshold frozen beforehand).

\section{Related Work}
The question is close to parameter control in evolutionary algorithms~\cite{EibenEtal1999,KarafotiasEtal2015} and to selection hyper-heuristics~\cite{BurkeEtal2013,DrakeEtal2020}, which choose among low-level heuristics online;
offline configurators such as irace~\cite{LopezIbanezEtal2016} and ParamILS~\cite{HutterEtal2009} fix the parameters before the run, and tuning and control are two ways of configuring the same algorithm~\cite{EibenSmit2011}.
Choosing among algorithms per instance is the algorithm selection problem~\cite{Rice1976}, whose libraries~\cite{BischlEtal2016} score a selector by the share of the gap between the single best solver and the per-instance oracle that it closes; our one-seed oracle is that oracle (here relative to random choice). What is specific here is that the oracle of a stochastic algorithm is inflated by run-to-run noise (hence cross-fitting) and that selection is learned online within a run.
Where learned neighbourhood selection is reported to beat strong baselines, the policy is trained offline~\cite{SonneratEtal2021}, as in our selector (Section~\ref{sec:selector}).
Adaptive operator selection (AOS)~\cite{FialhoEtal2010} uses probability matching~\cite{Goldberg1990}, adaptive pursuit~\cite{Thierens2005}, ALNS weights~\cite{PisingerRopke2007}, bandits~\cite{AuerEtal2002,DaCostaEtal2008}
or reinforcement learning with a state~\cite{BengioBengio2021}; the credit-assignment and selection rules of selection hyper-heuristics are the same family. Q-Learning has been used to select operators inside an IG for the permutation flowshop~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023} and, closest to this work,
the destruction size of an IG for identical machines with setups~\cite{MissaouiOSullivan2026}; both report that the learning-guided method beats its baselines. Guidelines for comparing
optimization algorithms~\cite{LaTorreEtal2021} and equivalence testing~\cite{Lakens2017} ground the protocol below.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Problem, instances and bounds}
In P$||\Cmax$, $n$ jobs with processing times $p_j$ are assigned to $m$ identical machines to minimise $\Cmax=\max_i\sum_{j\in S_i}p_j$; the problem is NP-hard.
The classical bound is $\mathrm{LB}=\max(\max_jp_j,\lceil\sum_jp_j/m\rceil)$~\cite{Graham1969}, and $\mathrm{GAP}=100(\Cmax-\mathrm{LB})/\mathrm{LB}$. A tighter, certified bound comes from the linear relaxation of the arc-flow
model~\cite{MradSouayah2018}; it equals the best makespan found in $75.3\%$ of the synthetic instances (proving optimality). The synthetic instances have $p_j\sim U[1,100]$ in twelve classes
$(n,m)$ from $(20,4)$ to $(300,20)$ (and $p_j\sim U[1,10^4]$ in one regime below); each class has $100$ test instances and $30$ separate tuning instances. With integer times one unit of makespan is $0.12$--$0.78\%$ of it (median $0.38\%$), so a mean difference of
$0.05\%$ averages many ties ($31$--$46\%$ of the instance pairs tie even after averaging five seeds). The published families B, C, E and F of the benchmark reviewed in~\cite{OstojicEtAl2025} give disjoint sets: $525$ tuning instances, $2{,}550$ \emph{discovery} and $3{,}950$ \emph{held-out}
test instances (families B, C, E), the $5{,}720$ B, C, E instances on which an offline selector is trained, and $780$ of family F (never used before); instances with times near $10^{18}$ do not fit the $64$-bit compiled core and were excluded ($378$ of the $7{,}403$ of the first three sets and $280$ of the $6{,}000$ training ones, all in family B).

\subsection{Search mechanisms and learners}
\label{sec:learners}
The \textbf{IG} starts from LPT (or from the best of LPT, MULTIFIT and LDM) and alternates destruction (removing $d$ jobs) and greedy reconstruction (non-increasing time onto the least-loaded machine), with greedy
acceptance $\Cmax(x')\leq\Cmax(x)$ and no separate perturbation phase. The \textbf{ILS} starts from LPT and, at each iteration, a selector chooses one of four neighbourhood operators (inter-machine swap,
reinsertion, intra-machine swap, block move) under best improvement; after $50$ iterations without improvement it restarts from the best solution. The \emph{actions} are the destruction strategy (random, longest jobs, machine-concentrated; $3$ actions), the destruction size
($d/n\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20,0.30,0.40\}$; $5$ actions), their $15$ combinations, these $15$ with or without a descent after reconstruction ($30$ actions), or the four ILS operators.
The reward of an iteration is the improvement of the makespan, truncated at zero. At each iteration a selector $\pi$ chooses an action from the state of the current solution (four features: gap to the bound, load imbalance, fraction of the budget used, stagnation), the IG destroys, reconstructs and optionally descends accordingly, $\pi$ receives the reward and the candidate is accepted if it does not worsen the makespan; the selector is the only component that changes between arms.
Two further hosts use the same selectors and protocol, in compiled code: the \textbf{permutation flowshop} with the IG of Ruiz and St\"utzle (NEH start, insertion local search, constant-temperature acceptance; the actions are the destruction size, alone or crossed with three temperature factors) a \textbf{steady-state genetic algorithm} for P$||\Cmax$ (population $20$, binary tournament, the child replaces the worst individual; an action is a crossover crossed with a mutation and one of two intensities) and the IG for \textbf{unrelated machines} (R$||\Cmax$; times $p_{jk}\sim U[1,100]$ drawn per machine, reconstruction by earliest completion).

The \emph{learners} are: tabular Q-Learning over $81$ states ($\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.9$,
$\varepsilon$-greedy from $1.0$ to $0.05$ by $0.995$ per step)~\cite{WatkinsDayan1992}; UCB1~\cite{AuerEtal2002}; a policy-gradient learner (softmax preferences per state, learning rate chosen on tuning instances)~\cite{Williams1992,SuttonBarto2018}; six methods without state, all tuned on the tuning instances: probability matching, adaptive pursuit, an ALNS-style roulette, Thompson sampling with discounting~\cite{Russo2018}, DMAB~\cite{DaCostaEtal2008} and an online racing learner; LinUCB on a continuous context~\cite{LiEtAl2010};
and a DQN with two layers of $64$ units, replay and a target network~\cite{MnihEtAl2015}. Random choice among the same actions is the control; Q-Learning itself is tuned per family only in Section~\ref{sec:selector}. The static competitor is the best fixed action per class,
chosen on the tuning instances.

\subsection{The budget-fair attribution protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\emph{Terms.} A \emph{class} is an $(n,m)$ pair, or a published family by size tercile; a \emph{cell} is a class in one action space (five or fifteen actions) and is the unit of the headroom analysis; the \emph{validated action} is the best fixed action per class, chosen on separate instances; the \emph{headroom} of a cell is the gain over random choice of the best fixed action per instance.
(1)~\emph{Random control at a matched budget:} the effect of the policy is the difference between the learner and uniform random choice over the same actions, iterations and instances.
(2)~\emph{Equal CPU time, calibrated sequentially:} for each pair, the CPU time ratio $K=\mathrm{CPU}_1/\mathrm{CPU}_2$ of $1000$ iterations of the first arm to $1000$ of the second is measured on separate instances, in sequence, with the process time on a machine kept as quiet as possible, and the second arm receives $\mathrm{round}(1000K)$ iterations; as $K$ is a property of the implementation, every arm must be implemented with comparable efficiency, and $K$ is reported. A learner's gain at equal CPU time is the sum of a
\emph{decision effect}, its gain at equal iterations, and a \emph{cost effect}, the extra iterations the random control loses by matching the CPU time of a learner that chooses cheaper actions: $G_{\mathrm{CPU}}=G_{\mathrm{dec}}+G_{\mathrm{cost}}$ with
$G_{\mathrm{dec}}=-\bar d_{\mathrm{iter}}$ and $G_{\mathrm{cost}}=\bar d_{\mathrm{iter}}-\bar d_{\mathrm{CPU}}$.
(3)~\emph{A static competitor validated offline} on separate instances is what a practitioner would use: the best fixed action per class, and, when actions are complementary, a uniform mixture of the best few. (4)~\emph{Cross-fitted oracle headroom:} the gain over random
choice of the best fixed action per instance estimates what a selector that picks one action per instance could gain; the action is chosen on two seeds and evaluated on two fresh ones, because the best of one seed per action carries run-to-run noise (a conservative estimate: the choice on two seeds is itself noisy) (Section~\ref{sec:published}). (5)~\emph{Power and a positive control:} the minimum detectable gain is estimated by planting gains in the paired differences (a fraction of the instances improved by one makespan unit),
and the learners and the pipeline are checked on a planted reward bonus and on a static policy chosen with class information. (6)~\emph{Statistics specified beforehand:} per instance (the mean of the seeds), $d=100(A-B)/B$ in the makespan is tested in pairs
(Wilcoxon, two-sided, ties dropped), with Holm correction over each family, a bootstrap $90\%$ interval, and an equivalence margin of $\pm0.05\%$, about an eighth of one makespan unit on the median synthetic instance; a Holm-significant difference whose interval lies inside
the margin is reported as parity, and one whose interval crosses the margin as a small win. The headline pairs are repeated with Pratt's treatment of ties and a paired permutation test, and the registered tests are pooled under a global Benjamini--Yekutieli control. Throughout the text and tables a \emph{gain} is $-\bar d$ in percentage points of the makespan (written ``points'' or $\%$), positive when the first-named arm is better. Hypotheses, decision rules and predictions were recorded, with md5 hashes of the code, in a dated diary before each battery was run.

\subsection{Implementations}
The local search was first written with Python loops, then vectorized (same moves, tie-breaking and results: $120$ of $120$ runs identical), then compiled with the IG, the destruction strategies and the agents (numba), exact on the deterministic parts and statistically equivalent on the random draws
(mean differences inside $\pm0.05\%$ in five checks); it also counts evaluations. The other hosts have their own compiled cores with the same agent code ($1{,}526$ unit tests in all).

\subsection{RQ1: the search structure and the budget, not the policy}
\label{sec:rq1}
\emph{Structure.} Against LPT and seven ILS variants in the twelve classes, the Q-Learning-guided IG is significantly better, after Holm correction, in $72$ of $96$ comparisons (Python study, equal iterations; the rest are not significant) and never worse. Yet its advantage over its four ablation siblings
(random selection, UCB1 with and without state, Q-Learning without state) is negligible: $0.011\%$ ($90\%$ interval $0.003$ to $0.019$; ten algorithm seeds on $240$ synthetic instances). In the earlier Python study, on two other sets of $4{,}160$ and $2{,}690$ published instances, it beats random selection of the destruction strategy by only $0.009$ to $0.029$ points (all six Holm-adjusted tests significant), and an informative state adds nothing
($-0.006$ points pooled, $p=0.77$). The mechanism is large: with learning removed from both sides, the IG beats the ILS at equal iterations (Python implementation) in $10$ of $12$ classes by $0.49\%$, against $0.53\%$ for the learned versions.
This does not depend on how efficiently the ILS is implemented: with vectorized operators ($6.2$ times faster; an iteration costing $0.4$ to $1.9$ times that of the IG), the IG still beats the ILS by $0.46\%$ at equal CPU time ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-80}$), with a positive gain in all twelve classes.

\emph{Budget.} Adding a short best-improvement descent after each reconstruction improves the IG by $0.150\%$ at an equal number of iterations, but it costs CPU time, so the verdict at equal CPU depends on the cost ratio $K$, and $K$ depends on the implementation.
With the first, loop-based descent ($K=9.6$) giving the plain IG the CPU time of the hybrid leaves an advantage of only $0.010\%$: $93\%$ of the gain would have been budget. Vectorizing the descent cuts $K$ to $1.8$ and the advantage at equal CPU is $0.105\%$
($30\%$ budget); compiling both arms gives $K=2.0$ and $0.115\%$ ($28\%$), a reading registered before the compiled run. For the practical recipe (tuned $d$, start from the best constructive solution) the descent is neutral at equal CPU time ($-0.008\%$ and $-0.006\%$, inside the margin). An evaluation count agrees in sign but not in size ($0.080\%$, $50\%$ budget); equal CPU time remains the budget of choice.

\subsection{RQ2: do learners beat random choice, does a static configuration win, and could learning have shown up?}
\label{sec:rq2}
Compiled runs are about a hundred times faster, which allows five seeds per instance (the unit of analysis is the mean of the five; $1{,}200$ pairs per row) in four action spaces
(Table~\ref{tab:agents}). A compiled agent adds little CPU time per evaluation (at most $1.33$ times that of random choice); $K$ in Table~\ref{tab:agents} is the cost of a whole run: $1.01$--$1.08$ for UCB1 and the policy gradient, $1.07$ for Q-Learning with three strategies and $0.50$--$0.60$ in the three larger spaces, because it learns to pick the cheaper actions. \emph{At an equal number of iterations none of the three original learners
gains more than $0.016\%$ over random choice in any of the $12$ comparisons}, within the equivalence margin (means over the $12$ classes; single cells differ, Fig.~\ref{fig:class}).
\emph{At equal CPU time Q-Learning does win} in the two spaces of sizes, by $0.059\%$ and $0.049\%$, \emph{but only by choosing cheaper actions}: its decision effect is nil ($-0.003$ and $-0.004$ points) and its cost effect is $0.062$ and $0.053$. Among the classic AOS methods only the ALNS-style roulette gains: $0.039$ points at equal iterations with five sizes
(inside the margin; $0.068$ at equal CPU time, outside it) and nothing at equal iterations with fifteen actions ($0.020$ at equal CPU time); the others stay within $0.013$ points, and online racing, the within-run analogue of a racing configurator, is \emph{worse} than random choice ($-0.012$ and $-0.067$ points).
The configuration validated per class is ahead of every one of the nine methods by $0.053$--$0.147$ points in the spaces of $5$ and $15$ actions ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-57}$)
and level with the three original learners in the other two, where the oracle headroom is smaller (``level'' means parity or no significant difference; it is $0.016$ points behind Q-Learning with three strategies). Where the actions differ in effect and cost (the four ILS operators, $1{,}200$ instances) a DQN and LinUCB lose to the uniform mixture ($0.041\%$ and $0.066\%$), as does the operator validated per class ($0.062\%$).

\emph{Could learning have shown up?}\label{sec:longnote} \emph{Power.} With $1{,}200$ pairs the analytic minimum detectable gain at $80\%$ power is $0.006$--$0.014$ points; planting gains in the paired differences gives, in the ten comparisons with a negligible observed effect, $58$--$99.8\%$ power at $0.02$ points and at least $97\%$ at $0.03$,
while the largest gain of Q-Learning, UCB1 or the policy gradient is $0.016$: gains of $0.03$ points or more are excluded for them. \emph{Positive controls.} The per-class validated action is detected at $0.092$ and $0.080$ points, and with a reward bonus planted on one action
all six learners tested choose it at least $1.5$ times more often than uniform. \emph{Horizon.} With five sizes and $T$ from $5{,}000$ to $100{,}000$ the learners stay within $0.006$ points of random choice while the one-seed oracle headroom (an upper bound) falls from $0.125\%$ at $T=1{,}000$ to $0.015\%$ at $T=100{,}000$, where the validated action still leads them by $0.012$--$0.017$ points ($p<10^{-12}$).
\emph{Finer times.} With $p_j\sim U[1,10^4]$ the cross-fitted headroom is $0.098$ and $0.121$ ($0.142$ and $0.175$ for one seed), yet no learner gains more than $0.015$ points at equal iterations and the validated action beats every learner by $0.105$--$0.136$ points. \emph{A time-varying competitor.} The best three-phase schedule of the five sizes per class, chosen on tuning instances, gains $0.095$ against $0.092$ for the validated action on P$||\Cmax$ (parity) and loses $0.166$ to it on unrelated machines ($0.178$ better on the tuning instances: the winner's curse among $125$ candidates): adapting over time adds nothing at the class level. \emph{A configurator.} irace~\cite{LopezIbanezEtal2016}, given the tuning cost of the validated action, tunes a continuous destruction size (and strategy) per class: parity with the validated action on P$||\Cmax$ (within $0.012$ points), $0.15$ and $0.19$ points behind on R$||\Cmax$, and still never ahead with ten times the budget. Tuning Q-Learning's four hyperparameters per class selects high exploration floors (median $0.26$--$0.37$) and beats the default Q-Learning on R$||\Cmax$ by $0.58$ and $0.63$ points, yet gains $0.01$ and $0.02$ over random choice on P$||\Cmax$ and $-0.45$ and $+0.17$ on R$||\Cmax$; the validated action beats it in all four host--space pairs ($0.06$--$1.90$ points).

\subsection{RQ3: where does an online selector pay, can that be foretold, and what exploits the headroom?}
\label{sec:published}\label{sec:selector}
\emph{The oracle headroom is mostly noise unless it is cross-fitted.} Taken as the best of one seed per action, the headroom of the synthetic classes is $0.125$ and $0.122$ points (five and fifteen actions); choosing the action on two seeds and evaluating it on two fresh ones removes half to two thirds of it ($0.063$ and $0.043$), and $28$--$40\%$ of the headroom of the published instances (discovery and held-out; held-out $0.132\to0.079$ and $0.174\to0.117$; only $15$--$20\%$ in family C, middle and upper terciles, $0.305\to0.244$ and $0.378\to0.321$ with five and fifteen actions).
On the flowshop, where run-to-run noise is high, it removes $80$--$90\%$ ($0.119\to0.012$ and $0.152\to0.025$). The cross-fitted value is a lower bound (two seeds still pick imperfectly) and the one-seed value an upper bound.

\emph{Published instances.} We ran the nine learners (compiled, five seeds, $T=1000$, five and fifteen actions) on $2{,}550$ of the published instances, with the hyperparameters and the validated action fixed per family and size tercile on $525$ separate ones. \emph{Here learners do beat random choice:}
Q-Learning gains $0.058$ points at equal iterations with fifteen actions and the ALNS roulette $0.057$ with five sizes; in family C, middle and upper size terciles, Q-Learning gains $0.16$ and $0.24$ points, while in family E it \emph{loses} $0.06$--$0.07$.
We fixed this reading as hypotheses before running $3{,}950$ held-out instances, retuning nothing. All five confirmatory tests held (Holm over five): Q-Learning gains $0.245$ and $0.149$ points in family C, middle and upper terciles, with fifteen and five actions, and loses $0.052$ and $0.059$ in family E; the ALNS roulette gains $0.056$ with five sizes. At equal CPU time, with its own cost ratio, the validated action is Holm-significantly ahead of the learner in all $54$ learner--space--set pairs, though only level (inside the margin) with the ALNS roulette with five sizes ($0.023$ and $0.024$).

\emph{A necessary condition, not a sufficient one.} The rank correlation between the cross-fitted headroom and Q-Learning's gain across the cells of a space is $0.95$ and $0.97$ on the held-out instances ($0.67$ and $0.60$ with the biased headroom) and $0.64$ and $0.66$ across the $12$ synthetic classes ($0.13$ and $0.01$ with it), but $-0.27$ and $-0.07$ on the flowshop ($-0.93$ and $-0.78$ before: that strong negative correlation was mostly noise). We adopted the cross-fitted estimator after the biased one failed in the synthetic classes and the flowshop.
One threshold, $h^*=0.048$ points, chosen on the $18$ discovery cells and applied unchanged to the $60$ others (held-out, synthetic and flowshop-synthetic cells), classifies $78\%$ of them correctly: balanced accuracy $0.80$ (bootstrap over the $30$ classes, each counted in both spaces: $95\%$ interval $0.67$--$0.91$, against $0.60$, $0.42$--$0.79$, with the one-seed headroom; $89\%$ held-out, $75\%$ synthetic, $72\%$ flowshop; Fig.~\ref{fig:class}). A post hoc check qualifies the rule: its excess over the $68\%$ of always predicting ``no gain'' is not significant; within the flowshop, where one cell in $18$ has a gain, it does not discriminate (balanced accuracy $0.38$); on the genetic algorithm ($24$ cells, outside the $60$) it classifies $88\%$; and in the $24$ cells with finer times it classifies only $58\%$ (a registered prediction that missed), because Q-Learning loses in the small classes despite a positive headroom.
The robust reading is one-sided (post hoc, the $150$ cells outside the discovery set, all hosts; Fig.~\ref{fig:class}): below $h^*$ there are $71$ cells and in none does Q-Learning gain beyond the margin (a point gain above $0.05$ points; $+0.026$ at most); above it there are $79$ others, and it does in $29$, gains something in $42$ and loses in $37$ (a median of $20\%$ of the headroom captured); with a margin of $0.02$ the counts are $2$ of $71$ and $37$ of $79$, with $0.10$ they are $0$ of $71$ and $15$ of $79$. The reading concerns Q-Learning and single-action headroom: a learner that mixes complementary actions can exceed it (Thompson sampling gains $0.051$ in the nine-action genetic algorithm, where the cross-fitted headroom is $-0.073$). Choosing the action on three seeds instead of two leaves the accuracy unchanged ($0.78$), and so does drawing the random-choice baselines of the headroom and of the gain from independent runs (balanced accuracy $0.80$, interval $0.68$--$0.90$): the estimate is conservative, and on the flowshop even the validated per-class action exceeds it.
\emph{Horizon.} In family C, held-out, middle and upper terciles (every third of the $1{,}020$ instances, $340$, with three seeds; $0.244$ for all $1{,}020$ at $T=1{,}000$), the cross-fitted headroom falls from $0.222$ points at $T=1{,}000$ to $0.047$ at $T=5{,}000$ and $0.023$ at $T=20{,}000$, Q-Learning's gain from $0.128$ to $0.033$ and $0.017$, and the validated action and Q-Learning become level (differences of $0.002$ and $0.001$ points, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}>0.9$): the headroom is a short-horizon phenomenon even where it is largest.

\emph{What exploits the headroom: a policy learned offline.} We trained an ExtraTrees regressor on $5{,}720$ B, C, E instances disjoint from every tuning and test set, with scale-free features of the instance ($n$, $m$, dispersion and quantiles of the times, bound ratios, the gap of LPT; no family label), to predict the relative performance of each fixed action; at test time it picks one action per instance.
The model and the hypotheses were fixed before any test run. On the discovery and held-out instances (Table~\ref{tab:selector}) it gains $0.024$--$0.044$ points over the validated action (inside the margin, hence parity, except in the larger space on held-out instances, where the $90\%$ interval crosses the margin), $0.08$--$0.095$ over Q-Learning and $0.04$--$0.14$ over the ALNS roulette at equal iterations, and captures $0.79$--$0.84$ of the one-seed oracle headroom (an upper bound) against $0.58$--$0.66$ for the validated action; in each of the four set--space pairs its gain over random choice ($0.099$--$0.146$ points) lies between the cross-fitted headroom (a lower bound) and the one-seed one. A control run afterwards qualifies the result: a per-class lookup table learned on the same instances (a class is a family, size group, $m$ and $n$) is level with the selector on the held-out instances, where every class was seen in training ($0.004$ and $0.006$ points, not significant), and the selector wins by only $0.014$ and $0.017$ on the discovery instances ($59\%$ of whose classes were seen): most of the gain is class-level knowledge.

\emph{Repairing the online learner.} Four attempts do not change the order. Tuning Q-Learning per family ($27$ settings of learning rate, discount and exploration decay) adds $0.009$--$0.014$ points; rewarding plateau moves, as the ALNS score does, adds $0.015$ with five sizes and \emph{costs} $0.007$--$0.009$ with fifteen; the validated action beats both in all eight comparisons, and the selector beats them by $0.06$--$0.10$. A Q table \emph{pre-trained} offline on the same instances is no better than a cold one with five sizes
($-0.006$ and $+0.005$ points) and much worse with fifteen ($-0.146$ and $-0.143$; after $5.7$ million updates its table is mostly zeros, and breaking ties at random does not help). Optimistic initialization stays level with random choice ($0.004$--$0.008$ points), is worse than the cold Q-Learning ($0.007$--$0.051$) and loses to the validated action by $0.067$--$0.104$. The limit is the learning signal (bandit feedback), not the cold start: the selector sees every action's outcome on every training instance.

\subsection{Other hosts: a flowshop, a genetic algorithm and unrelated machines}
\label{sec:flowshop}
We repeated the protocol, in compiled code, on the permutation flowshop (nine classes, $n\in\{20,50,100\}$, $m\in\{5,10,20\}$, with $20$ tuning and $40$ test instances each, plus the $90$ instances ta001--ta090 of Taillard; $T=1000$, three seeds; five and fifteen actions), on a steady-state genetic algorithm for P$||\Cmax$ ($1{,}200$ instances, $T=10{,}000$ offspring, nine and eighteen actions) and on unrelated machines; their gains are in Table~\ref{tab:hosts} and their cells (except the last host's) in Fig.~\ref{fig:class}.
\emph{Flowshop.} The cross-fitted headroom is only $0.012$ and $0.025$ points. No online learner beats random choice at equal iterations (Q-Learning loses $0.05$--$0.08$ points, and $0.03$--$0.05$ at equal CPU time) and the validated action beats the five learners in all ten learner--space pairs of the synthetic instances at equal iterations (by $0.020$--$0.104$).
\emph{Genetic algorithm.} The actions are complementary, so a single validated action can lose to uniform random choice ($-0.057$ points with nine actions). Thompson sampling gains $0.051$ and $0.047$ points, Q-Learning loses $0.096$ and $0.048$, and the competitor that wins is a validated \emph{mixture}, the uniform choice among the three best single actions of each class (specified after the single action lost on this host): it gains $0.050$ and $0.144$ points, beats the validated single action by $0.105$ and $0.067$ and nine of the ten learner--space pairs at equal iterations and at equal CPU time.
\emph{Unrelated machines (R$||\Cmax$).} The synthetic classes sit near the ceiling, so we added a host with real headroom, tested with the threshold frozen and the hypotheses registered beforehand (the same twelve classes, $100$ test instances each, $p_{jk}\sim U[1,100]$; predictions fixed after a pilot on tuning instances). The one-seed headroom is $1.5$ and $2.8$ points (cross-fitted $0.43$ and $1.60$), $12$ and $23$ times that of the synthetic P$||\Cmax$ ($7$ and $37$ times cross-fitted), and the validated action gains $0.95$ and $2.08$ points. Yet Q-Learning loses ($-1.06$ and $-0.48$), UCB1 and the policy gradient are at parity with random choice, and, in the main battery, only the optimistic Q-Learning ($+0.17$, five sizes) and the ALNS roulette ($+0.15$, fifteen actions) gain beyond the margin, at most $18\%$ and $7\%$ of the validated action's gain; the validated action beats every learner in all ten learner--space pairs, by $0.77$--$2.52$ points (paired, each relative to the learner). An offline selector trained on $1{,}200$ further instances ($18{,}000$ and $54{,}000$ training runs, five times the tuning of the validated action) beats the best online learner by $0.72$ and $1.91$ points but captures $60\%$ and $73\%$ of the headroom, no more than the validated action ($-0.056$ and $-0.023$ points; level with the class table): instance-level information adds nothing again. Two published AOS designs, tuned per space on the tuning instances as their hyperparameters were not available (an episodic Q-Learning with a binary state in the style of~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023} and an FRRMAB-style sliding-window bandit), do not close the distance: on P$||\Cmax$ neither gains beyond the margin; on R$||\Cmax$ the first loses $0.15$ and the second gains $0.02$ with five sizes, and they gain $0.24$ and $0.29$ with fifteen actions ($14\%$ of the validated action's gain at most), which beats both in all eight comparisons ($0.06$--$1.82$ points). The frozen rule is not a classifier here ($42\%$ of the $24$ cells: $19$ lie above $h^*$ and Q-Learning gains in only $5$ of those), but its one-sided reading holds out of sample: none of the $5$ cells below $h^*$ shows a gain beyond the margin.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The gains that learning-guided search reports are mostly structure (destruction--reconstruction), budget (and how efficiently it is spent) and tuning. A learner's own contribution, the decision effect, is bounded by one quantity, the headroom of the best fixed action net of run-to-run noise: below about $0.05$ points the online learner never gains beyond the margin, above it it sometimes recovers part of what a validated action gives (family C, the upper size terciles) and often loses (small classes, finer times, the flowshop), and even where the headroom is large (unrelated machines) it captures a small fraction, published designs included. What headroom rewards is knowledge acquired before the run (a selector trained offline captures about four fifths of the one-seed oracle gain, mostly class-level knowledge); where the actions are complementary (a genetic algorithm, the ILS operators) the static competitor must be a \emph{mixture}.
\emph{A rule of use.} Estimate the cross-fitted headroom on tuning instances (the gain over random choice of the best fixed action per instance, chosen on some seeds and evaluated on others) or its cheap proxy, the validated-gain-to-noise ratio (the cross-fitted gain of the best fixed action over the noise of random choice; AUC $0.75$ and $0.73$ for ``Q-Learning gains'' on the $26$ classes of an unseen family, F): below about $0.05$ points an online selector does not pay, and in all cases a validated action or mixture, or better a policy trained offline, is the baseline to beat.

\emph{What the registered predictions showed.} The batteries were specified before they were run; $221$ of $278$ point predictions held in $35$ batteries. The misses (cost ratios, the classic AOS methods, the published instances, the flowshop, warm-started and optimistic Q-Learning, the genetic algorithm, finer times) led to the headroom rule and to cross-fitting; the largest correction they forced on our text is the $93\%$ budget share, registered with the loop-based descent, that fell to $30\%$ once every arm was implemented with comparable efficiency. The headline pairs keep their significance under Pratt's treatment of ties and a permutation test, and $829$ of the $855$ confirmed tests survive a global Benjamini--Yekutieli control over the $1{,}562$ tests registered up to the genetic-algorithm battery.

\emph{Limitations.} (i) The equal-CPU comparison depends on $K$, measured on separate instances in sequence (not always on a quiet machine) and reported for every arm; it was not run on unrelated machines. (ii) Gains are in points of the makespan, so $0.05\%$ may or may not matter, and the margin is a choice; as an absolute reference, the IG with random choice reaches the classical lower bound in $42$--$48\%$ of the synthetic runs (mean gap $0.26$--$0.28\%$) and is within $0.2$--$1.3\%$ of it on the published families, and we did not compare it with the solvers reviewed in~\cite{OstojicEtAl2025}: the protocol compares arms, not absolute quality.
(iii) Each battery has its own Holm family and registration; the headroom rule began as an exploratory reading, was confirmed on instances that took no part in it and rests on few cells per space and host (the threshold was chosen on the discovery cells; the rule does not discriminate within the flowshop, at finer times or on unrelated machines, and the base-rate, bootstrap and one-sided readings were post hoc). (iv) The evidence is for P$||\Cmax$, R$||\Cmax$ with uncorrelated times, the permutation flowshop and one genetic algorithm.
(v) State-dependent oracles, deep policy gradients and portfolios of hundreds of operators are outside the evidence, horizons up to $T=10^5$ were studied with five sizes only, the cross-fitted headroom is a conservative, downward-biased estimate, and the offline selector's training cost is not charged to it ($85{,}800$ and $257{,}400$ IG runs, about $0.7$ CPU-hours for both spaces, or $21{,}700$ and $65{,}200$ extra iterations per held-out instance if amortized, a horizon at which the headroom has largely vanished). (vi) The pre-specification is a dated, hash-stamped diary under version control, not a public registry.

\section{Conclusion}
A learned operator selector is not what explains the advantage of an IG over ILS for identical parallel machines: that belongs to destruction--reconstruction, and the share of a local search's gain that is only budget is $93\%$, $30\%$ or $28\%$ depending on whether it is implemented with loops, vectorized or compiled, so the CPU cost ratio is itself a result to report.
The oracle headroom net of run-to-run noise is a necessary condition for an online selector to pay, not a sufficient one: no cell below about $0.05$ points gains beyond the margin, while above it Q-Learning gains $0.15$--$0.24$ points in the middle and upper size terciles of family C of the published benchmark (confirmed on $3{,}950$ held-out instances) and loses in the small classes; on unrelated machines, with $12$--$23$ times the headroom, the best online learner still gets at most $18\%$ of what a validated action gains. A static configuration (validated offline, or a validated mixture) was never worse than the online learner beyond the equivalence margin, and a selector trained offline exploits the headroom, mostly through class-level knowledge;
tuning, a plateau-aware reward, a pre-trained table and optimistic initialization do not repair the online learner, whose limit is its feedback. Future work: contextual bandits with full feedback and larger portfolios.
