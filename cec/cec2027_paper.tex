\begin{abstract}
Learning-guided metaheuristics are usually reported to beat non-learning counterparts, but studies seldom separate what the policy adds from what the search structure, the CPU budget and the tuning already give.
We propose a budget-fair attribution protocol (random control, equal CPU time, a static configuration chosen offline, a power check, a positive control) and apply it to Iterated Greedy (IG) for parallel machine scheduling, with a flowshop, a genetic algorithm, unrelated machines and a third-party ALNS library as checks.
Three findings. (1) The IG's advantage over iterated local search comes from its greedy reconstruction, not from learning: a classical ILS stays $0.6$--$0.8$ percentage points of makespan behind at equal CPU time, while online learning adds at most $0.04$.
(2) A static action chosen on separate instances is the baseline to beat: a Q-Learning variant beat it in $2$ of $69$ cells (by under $0.02$ points), and no selector of the library did in $40$ comparisons.
(3) The standard Q-Learning repeats the action listed first, so its gains follow the order of the list; with ties broken at random it recovers about $16\%$ of the static action's gain, in step with it (rank correlation $0.78$).
Gains come from knowledge acquired offline, not from learning during the run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Metaheuristics that learn while they search are a prominent line of work in evolutionary computation and operations research: adaptive operator selection (AOS)~\cite{FialhoEtal2010,AletiMoser2016}, selection hyper-heuristics~\cite{BurkeEtal2013} and \emph{learnheuristics}~\cite{CalvetEtal2017}, which couple a search with machine learning.
A typical design embeds a reinforcement-learning agent, most often tabular Q-Learning~\cite{WatkinsDayan1992,SuttonBarto2018}, as a selector of operators, strategies or parameters inside a framework such as Iterated Local Search (ILS)~\cite{LourencaoEtal2019} or Iterated Greedy (IG)~\cite{RuizStutzle2007}, and reports that it beats the search without the agent or published baselines~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023,MissaouiOSullivan2026}.
Such results are read as evidence that the \emph{learned policy} is what helps.

Three confounds make that reading fragile. First, the \emph{search structure} that hosts the agent (which operators, which acceptance rule, whether there is a local search) may produce most of the gain under any selection rule.
Second, the \emph{budget}: an agent changes the CPU time per iteration and a local search multiplies it, so equal iteration counts are not equal budgets.
Third, the \emph{tuning}: a configuration validated offline on separate instances is a competitor that most studies omit.
A survey of parameter control already points out methodological shortcomings~\cite{KarafotiasEtal2015}; we make the controls explicit, measurable and reusable, and show that a detail of the standard Q-Learning, not learning, can produce apparent wins.

We ask three questions. \textbf{RQ1:} what explains the advantage of a learning-guided IG over ILS, and how much of a local search's gain is only budget?
\textbf{RQ2:} do online learners beat random choice over the same actions, at equal iterations and at equal CPU time; does a static configuration win anyway; and could learning have shown up?
\textbf{RQ3:} where, if anywhere, does an online selector pay, and what exploits what it could learn?
Our contributions are (i) a \emph{budget-fair attribution protocol} (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), released as a small library; (ii) evidence that the IG's advantage comes from its greedy reconstruction, that the share of a local search's gain that is only budget depends on the implementation, and that the wins of the standard Q-Learning come from repeating the action listed first; and (iii) an empirical regularity: a Q-Learning that does not lock in gains in step with the gain of the static action but recovers only a small share of it, whereas a policy learned offline recovers more.

\section{Related Work}
The question is close to parameter control in evolutionary algorithms~\cite{KarafotiasEtal2015,AletiMoser2016} and to selection hyper-heuristics~\cite{BurkeEtal2013}, which choose among low-level heuristics online (HyFlex~\cite{OchoaEtal2012} is a standard testbed, and simple learning rules can provably pay in some settings~\cite{AlanaziLehre2014});
offline configurators such as irace~\cite{LopezIbanezEtal2016} fix the parameters before the run. Surveys map the use of machine learning in metaheuristics~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2022}, and guidelines for benchmarking standardise comparisons between algorithms~\cite{BartzBeielsteinEtal2020}; they do not attribute a gain to its source.
Choosing among algorithms per instance is the algorithm selection problem~\cite{Rice1976}, whose libraries~\cite{BischlEtal2016} score a selector by the share of the gap between the single best solver and the per-instance oracle that it closes (our one-seed oracle); for a stochastic algorithm that oracle is inflated by run-to-run noise, hence cross-fitting.
Where learned neighbourhood selection beats strong baselines, the policy is trained offline~\cite{SonneratEtal2021}, as in our selector (Section~\ref{sec:selector}).

AOS~\cite{FialhoEtal2010} uses probability matching~\cite{Goldberg1990}, adaptive pursuit~\cite{Thierens2005}, ALNS weights~\cite{PisingerRopke2007}, bandits~\cite{AuerEtal2002,DaCostaEtal2008} or reinforcement learning with a state~\cite{SharmaEtal2019}; the credit-assignment and selection rules of selection hyper-heuristics are the same family.
Q-Learning has been used to select operators inside an IG for the permutation flowshop~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023} and, for parallel machines with weighted earliness--tardiness (a different objective), to select the destruction size of an IG~\cite{MissaouiOSullivan2026}; both report that the learning-guided method beats its baselines.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Problem, instances and bounds}
In P$||\Cmax$, $n$ jobs with times $p_j$ are assigned to $m$ identical machines to minimise $\Cmax=\max_i\sum_{j\in S_i}p_j$.
The classical bound is $\mathrm{LB}=\max(\max_jp_j,\lceil\sum_jp_j/m\rceil)$~\cite{Graham1969}; a tighter, certified bound from the linear relaxation of an arc-flow model~\cite{MradSouayah2018} equals the best makespan found in $75.3\%$ of the synthetic instances (proving optimality).
The synthetic instances have $p_j\sim U[1,100]$ in twelve classes $(n,m)$ with $n/m$ from $3$ to $15$ (Table~\ref{tab:classes}); each class has $100$ test and $30$ separate tuning instances.
With integer times one unit of makespan is $0.12$--$0.78\%$ of it (median $0.38\%$), so a mean difference of $0.05\%$ averages many ties ($31$--$46\%$ of the instance pairs tie after averaging five seeds), and the classes sit close to the ceiling: random choice is at the bound in $7$--$96\%$ of the runs.
The published families B, C and E of the benchmark reviewed in~\cite{OstojicEtAl2025} give disjoint sets: $525$ tuning, $2{,}550$ \emph{discovery} and $3{,}950$ \emph{held-out} test instances, and $5{,}720$ on which an offline selector is trained (a few instances with times near $10^{18}$ do not fit the $64$-bit compiled core and were excluded).

\subsection{Search mechanisms and learners}
\label{sec:learners}
The \textbf{IG} starts from LPT (or from the best of LPT, MULTIFIT and LDM) and alternates destruction (removing $d$ jobs) and greedy reconstruction (non-increasing time onto the least-loaded machine); a candidate is accepted if it does not worsen $\Cmax$.
The \textbf{ILS} starts from LPT; at each iteration a selector chooses one of four neighbourhood operators (inter-machine swap, reinsertion, intra-machine swap, block move), applied under best improvement; after $50$ iterations without improvement it returns to the best solution and applies three random moves.
The \emph{actions} are the destruction strategy (random, longest jobs, machine-concentrated; $3$ actions), the destruction size ($d/n\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20,0.30,0.40\}$; $5$ actions), their $15$ combinations, these $15$ with or without a short descent after reconstruction ($30$ actions), or the four ILS operators.
At each iteration a selector $\pi$ chooses an action from the state of the current solution (four features: gap to the bound, load imbalance, fraction of the budget used, stagnation; $81$ discrete states), the IG acts, and $\pi$ receives the reward, the improvement of the makespan truncated at zero; the selector is the only component that changes between arms.
Further hosts use the same selectors and protocol in compiled code: a \textbf{permutation flowshop} IG~\cite{RuizStutzle2007} (NEH start, insertion local search, constant-temperature acceptance; the actions are the destruction size, alone or crossed with three temperature factors); a \textbf{steady-state genetic algorithm} for P$||\Cmax$ (population $20$, binary tournament, the child replaces the worst individual; an action is a crossover crossed with a mutation and one of two intensities); the IG for \textbf{unrelated machines} (R$||\Cmax$; $p_{jk}\sim U[1,100]$ per machine, reconstruction by earliest completion), also on the published families of Fanjul-Peyro and Ruiz; and, as a third-party host, the ALNS of the \texttt{alns} library~\cite{WoudaLan2023} for the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP), with its own selectors.

The \emph{learners} are tabular Q-Learning ($\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.9$, $\varepsilon$-greedy from $1.0$ to $0.05$ by $\times0.995$ per step, zero-initialised table, ties broken by the lowest action index)~\cite{WatkinsDayan1992}; UCB1~\cite{AuerEtal2002} (exploration constant $\sqrt2$) and a policy-gradient learner (softmax preferences per state; learning rate in $\{0.03,0.1,0.3\}$ chosen on the tuning instances)~\cite{Williams1992,SuttonBarto2018}, both with the reward divided by the largest improvement seen so far; six methods without state, tuned on the tuning instances: probability matching, adaptive pursuit, an ALNS-style roulette, Thompson sampling with discounting~\cite{Russo2018}, DMAB~\cite{DaCostaEtal2008} and an online racing learner; LinUCB on a continuous context~\cite{LiEtAl2010}; and a DQN with two layers of $64$ units, replay and a target network~\cite{MnihEtAl2015}.
With $81$ states and $1000$ decisions a cell of the Q table of a five-action space is visited $2.5$ times on average, which is why learners without state ($\approx200$ pulls per action) are included.
The \emph{static competitor} is the best fixed action per class, chosen on the tuning instances.

\subsection{The budget-fair attribution protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\emph{Terms.} A \emph{class} is an $(n,m)$ pair, a published family by size tercile, or a type of the R$||\Cmax$ benchmark of Fanjul-Peyro and Ruiz; a \emph{cell} is a class in one action space (five or fifteen actions, and also thirty for the benchmark) and is the unit of the cell analysis of Section~\ref{sec:published}: $24$ synthetic and $18$ held-out published P$||\Cmax$ cells, $21$ benchmark and $6$ published-family R$||\Cmax$ cells, $69$ in all.
The \emph{validated action} is the best fixed action per class, chosen on separate tuning instances. The \emph{gain} of a method is $-\bar d$ in percentage points of the makespan (written ``points'' or $\%$) over random choice of the same actions at equal iterations, positive when the method is better; its \emph{capture} is its gain divided by the validated action's.
The \emph{cross-fitted oracle headroom} is the gain over random choice of the best fixed action per instance, the action being chosen on two seeds and evaluated on two fresh ones (a lower bound; the best of one seed per action is an upper bound).
(1)~\emph{Random control at a matched budget:} the effect of the policy is the difference between the learner and uniform random choice over the same actions, iterations and instances.
(2)~\emph{Equal CPU time, calibrated sequentially:} the CPU ratio $K=\mathrm{CPU}_1/\mathrm{CPU}_2$ of $1000$ iterations of the first arm to $1000$ of the second is measured on separate instances, and the second arm receives $\mathrm{round}(1000K)$ iterations; $K$ is a property of the implementation and is reported. A learner's gain at equal CPU time is the sum of a \emph{decision effect}, its gain at equal iterations, and a \emph{cost effect}, what the random control loses by matching the CPU time of a learner that chooses cheaper actions.
(3)~\emph{A static competitor validated offline} on separate instances is what a practitioner would use: the best fixed action per class and, when actions are complementary, a uniform mixture of the best few; its tuning is priced in Section~\ref{sec:selector}.
(4)~\emph{Cross-fitted headroom,} defined above, because the best of one seed per action carries run-to-run noise.
(5)~\emph{Power and a positive control:} the minimum detectable gain is estimated by planting gains in the paired differences, and the learners and the pipeline are checked on a planted reward bonus and on a static policy chosen with class information.
(6)~\emph{Statistics specified beforehand:} per instance (the mean of the seeds), $d=100(A-B)/B$ is tested in pairs (Wilcoxon, two-sided, ties dropped) with Holm correction over each family of comparisons, with a bootstrap $90\%$ interval of the mean and an equivalence margin~\cite{Lakens2017} of $\pm0.05\%$, about an eighth of one makespan unit on the median synthetic instance.
A difference is \emph{beyond the margin} when it is Holm-significant and its interval is not inside $\pm0.05$ (its point estimate may be smaller); a Holm-significant difference whose interval lies inside the margin is parity (the criterion of two one-sided tests).
Hypotheses, decision rules and predictions were recorded, with md5 hashes of the code, in a dated internal log before each battery was run; \emph{registered} below means specified there beforehand and \emph{exploratory} not (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Implementations}
The local search was first written with Python loops, then vectorized ($120$ of $120$ runs identical), then compiled together with the IG, the destruction strategies and the agents (numba), exact on the deterministic parts and statistically equivalent on the random draws (inside $\pm0.05\%$ in five checks); the other hosts have their own compiled cores with the same agent code ($1{,}606$ unit tests in all). All runs used one laptop (Intel Core i5-12450H, 8 cores, 16~GB), where $K$ was measured.

\subsection{RQ1: the search structure and the budget, not the policy}
\label{sec:rq1}
\emph{Structure.} Against LPT and seven ILS variants in the twelve classes, the Q-Learning-guided IG is significantly better, after Holm correction, in $72$ of $96$ comparisons (Python study, equal iterations) and never worse.
Yet its advantage over its four ablation siblings (random selection, UCB1 with and without state, Q-Learning without state) is only $0.011\%$ ($90\%$ interval $0.003$ to $0.019$; ten seeds on $240$ instances).
With learning removed from both sides, the IG beats the ILS in $10$ of $12$ classes (the other two differ by less than $0.01\%$) by $0.49\%$, against $0.53\%$ for the learned versions: Q-Learning helps the IG by $0.011$ and hurts the ILS by about $0.03$ points.
This does not depend on how efficiently the ILS is implemented: with vectorized operators ($6.2$ times faster; an iteration costing $0.4$ to $1.9$ times that of the IG) the IG still wins by $0.46\%$ at equal CPU time, with a positive gain in all twelve classes.

\emph{Which part of the structure?} The ILS above perturbs weakly, so Table~\ref{tab:ablation} crosses the two components of the compiled IG, in the five-size space with the action drawn at random (no learner): greedy reconstruction or random reinsertion of the removed jobs, with no descent, the IG's short descent (at most two improving moves) or a descent to a local optimum; random reinsertion with the full descent is the classical ILS for P$||\Cmax$ (R2).
Replacing the greedy reconstruction by random reinsertion costs $1.7$ points without a descent (in all twelve classes, from $0.10$ to $5.3$; $1.7$ at equal CPU time), $0.9$ with the short one and $0.7$ with the full one (nine classes), more than forty times the largest gain of a learner over random choice in the same space ($0.039$, Table~\ref{tab:agents}).
The plain IG beats the classical ILS by $0.65$ points at equal iterations and $0.79$ at equal CPU time (the ILS costs $5$--$13$ times as much per run), and still by $0.45$ and $0.57$ when the ILS uses the best fixed kick size of each class, chosen on the test instances in its favour (it is ahead in three small classes at equal iterations, never at equal CPU time).
A full descent adds nothing to the greedy reconstruction ($+0.003$), which already sits near a local optimum ($0.9$ moves per descent against $5.7$ after a random reinsertion), and little to random reinsertion ($+0.16$). The reconstruction, not the policy, is the active component.

\emph{Budget.} A short best-improvement descent after each reconstruction improves the IG (random choice among the three destruction strategies, $d=0.3n$) by $0.150\%$ at an equal number of iterations, but it costs CPU time, so the verdict at equal CPU depends on the cost ratio $K$, and $K$ depends on the implementation.
With the first, loop-based descent ($K=9.6$) the advantage at equal CPU is only $0.010\%$ ($93\%$ of the gain would have been budget); vectorized ($K=1.8$) it is $0.105\%$ ($30\%$); compiled ($K=2.0$), $0.115\%$ of the $0.159\%$ at equal iterations ($28\%$; a reading registered before the compiled run).

\subsection{RQ2: do learners beat random choice, does a static configuration win, and could learning have shown up?}
\label{sec:rq2}
Compiled runs are about a hundred times faster, which allows five seeds per instance in four IG action spaces (Table~\ref{tab:agents}).
\emph{At equal iterations none of the three original learners gains more than $0.016$ points in any of the $12$ comparisons}, within the equivalence margin (means over the $12$ classes).
\emph{At equal CPU time Q-Learning does win} in the two larger spaces, by $0.059$ and $0.049$, \emph{but only through the cost effect} ($0.062$ and $0.053$; decision effect $-0.003$ and $-0.004$): it repeats the action listed first, the cheapest, so $K=0.50$--$0.60$ (Section~\ref{sec:published}).
Among the classic AOS methods only the ALNS-style roulette gains ($0.039$ at equal iterations with five sizes, inside the margin; $0.068$ at equal CPU time, outside it); the others, racing aside, stay within $0.013$, and online racing is \emph{worse} than random choice ($-0.012$ and $-0.067$).
The configuration validated per class is ahead of each of the nine methods by $0.053$--$0.147$ points with $5$ and $15$ actions and level with the three original learners in the other two spaces, where the headroom is smaller.
Where the actions differ in effect and cost (the four ILS operators, $1{,}200$ instances) a DQN and LinUCB lose to the uniform mixture ($0.041$ and $0.066$), as does the operator validated per class ($0.062$).

\emph{Could learning have shown up?}\label{sec:longnote} \emph{Power.} With $1{,}200$ pairs the analytic minimum detectable gain at $80\%$ power is $0.006$--$0.014$ points; planting gains in the paired differences gives, in the ten comparisons with a negligible observed effect, $58$--$99.8\%$ power at $0.02$ points and at least $97\%$ at $0.03$, while the largest gain of Q-Learning, UCB1 or the policy gradient is $0.016$: gains of $0.03$ or more are excluded for them.
\emph{Positive controls.} The validated action is detected at $0.092$ and $0.080$ points. With a reward bonus planted on one action all six learners tested choose it $1.5$--$13.7$ times as often as uniform choice would, and with a bonus that depends on the state Q-Learning and the policy gradient choose the best action $3.8$ and $2.5$ times as often (five actions).
\emph{Horizon and finer times.} With $T$ up to $10^5$ the learners stay within $0.006$ points of random choice on P$||\Cmax$, where the one-seed headroom falls to $0.015\%$; on unrelated machines Q-Learning never beats random choice and the validated action beats every learner at every horizon ($0.37$--$2.33$). With finer times ($p_j\sim U[1,10^4]$) no learner gains more than $0.015$.

\emph{Competitors beyond the main battery.} irace~\cite{LopezIbanezEtal2016}, given the tuning cost of the validated action, tunes a continuous destruction size and strategy per class: parity with the validated action on P$||\Cmax$ (within $0.012$) and $0.15$--$0.19$ behind on R$||\Cmax$, never ahead beyond the margin even with ten times the budget.
Two published AOS designs, re-implemented and tuned per space as their hyperparameters were not available (an episodic Q-Learning with a binary state in the style of~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023} and an FRRMAB-style sliding-window bandit~\cite{LiFialhoEtal2014}), gain nothing beyond the margin on P$||\Cmax$ and at most $14\%$ of the validated action's gain on R$||\Cmax$; the validated action beats both in all eight comparisons ($0.06$--$1.82$).

\subsection{RQ3: where does an online selector pay, and what exploits what it could learn?}
\label{sec:published}\label{sec:selector}
\emph{What there is to learn.} Taken as the best of one seed per action, the oracle headroom of the synthetic classes is $0.125$ and $0.122$ points (five and fifteen actions); choosing the action on two seeds and evaluating it on two fresh ones removes half to two thirds of it ($0.063$ and $0.043$), and $28$--$40\%$ of that of the published instances: mostly run-to-run noise.
Cross-fitting is conservative, not a bound: the gain of the validated action exceeds the cross-fitted headroom in $41$ of the $60$ P$||\Cmax$ cells. We therefore measure what a learner finds against what a static action finds offline, its capture.

\emph{Published instances: a reading and its qualification.} We ran the nine learners on $2{,}550$ published instances, with the hyperparameters and the validated action fixed per family and size tercile on $525$ separate ones. The standard Q-Learning seemed to beat random choice: it gains $0.058$ points at equal iterations with fifteen actions and, in family C, middle and upper size terciles, $0.16$ and $0.24$, while in family E it \emph{loses} $0.06$--$0.07$.
We fixed this reading as hypotheses before running $3{,}950$ held-out instances, retuning nothing, and all five confirmatory tests held (family C: $+0.245$ and $+0.149$; family E: $-0.052$ and $-0.059$; ALNS roulette with five sizes: $+0.056$).
At equal CPU time the validated action is Holm-significantly ahead of the learner in all $54$ comparisons (nine learners, two spaces, three instance sets), though only level with the ALNS roulette with five sizes ($0.023$ and $0.024$).

\emph{An implementation artefact: the order of the actions.} The standard Q-Learning (zero-initialised table, non-negative reward, ties broken by the first index) repeats the action listed first. On the R$||\Cmax$ benchmark of Fanjul-Peyro and Ruiz it puts $55$--$73\%$ of its choices on it, while UCB1 and the policy gradient stay at the share of random choice; reversing the list moves the lock-in to the new first action ($63$--$72\%$) and listing the validated action first recovers $81$--$86\%$ of its gain (Fig.~\ref{fig:class}c).
On the synthetic classes the gain of the standard Q-Learning is the gain of its first action: across $48$ cells (twelve classes, four action spaces) their rank correlation is $0.94$ (median absolute difference $0.04$ points), against $0.03$ and $0.07$ for UCB1 and the policy gradient.
Our lists run from the smallest to the largest destruction, so the first action is the cheapest, which explains the wins at equal CPU time on P$||\Cmax$. Permuting the list at random once per run does not make the learner order-invariant, since it only picks one random action to repeat: it gains beyond the margin in none of the $69$ cells and loses $0.25$ and $0.12$ points in family C, middle and upper terciles, where the standard learner gained $0.245$ and $0.149$.
Breaking ties at random at every decision removes the lock-in.

\emph{What a learner that does not lock in finds.} With ties broken at random Q-Learning gains beyond the margin in $25$ of the $69$ cells (all six published R$||\Cmax$ families, $15$ of $21$ benchmark cells, $4$ of $18$ published P$||\Cmax$, none of the $24$ synthetic ones; $29$ and $19$ cells with margins of $0.02$ and $0.10$), but it captures a small share of the validated gain: $0.16$ pooled over the cells (90\% interval by class $0.14$--$0.19$), and $0.06$, $0.16$, $0.17$ and $0.28$ in the synthetic, benchmark, family and published P$||\Cmax$ cells.
Its gain follows the validated action's, with a rank correlation of $0.78$ over the cells (interval by class $0.57$--$0.89$; $0.49$--$0.94$ within each collection), against $-0.06$ for the standard learner and $-0.64$ for the permuted one, and in none of the $18$ cells where the validated gain is at most $0.05$ does it gain beyond the margin (Fig.~\ref{fig:class}a--b).
The validated action beats the random-tie learner in $59$ of the $69$ cells and is beaten in $2$, by less than $0.02$ points, where it does not itself gain. We could not verify whether published implementations lock in: the extension of~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023} initialises its Q table with zeros and takes the argmax without stating a tie rule~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2024}.

\emph{What exploits it: a policy learned offline.} We trained an ExtraTrees regressor on $5{,}720$ B, C, E instances disjoint from every tuning and test set, with scale-free features of the instance (size, dispersion of the times, bound ratios, the gap of LPT; no family label), to predict the relative performance of each fixed action; at test time it picks one action per instance. The model and the hypotheses were fixed before any test run.
On the discovery and held-out instances (Table~\ref{tab:selector}) it gains $0.024$--$0.044$ points over the validated action (parity, except in the larger space on held-out instances, where the interval crosses the margin), $0.08$--$0.095$ over Q-Learning and $0.04$--$0.14$ over the ALNS roulette, and captures $0.79$--$0.84$ of the one-seed headroom (an upper bound) against $0.58$--$0.66$ for the validated action.
A control run afterwards qualifies the result: a per-class lookup table learned on the same instances is level with the selector on the held-out instances, where every class was seen in training ($0.004$ and $0.006$ points, not significant), and the selector wins by only $0.017$ and $0.014$ on the discovery instances ($59\%$ of whose classes were seen): most of the gain is class-level knowledge.

\emph{Repairing the online learner.} Four attempts do not change the order. Tuning Q-Learning per family adds $0.009$--$0.014$ points; rewarding plateau moves, as the ALNS score does, adds $0.015$ with five sizes and \emph{costs} $0.007$--$0.009$ with fifteen; a Q table \emph{pre-trained} offline is no better than a cold one with five sizes and much worse with fifteen ($-0.146$ and $-0.143$); optimistic initialization stays level with random choice ($0.004$--$0.008$).
The validated action beats the tuned and the plateau-reward learners in all eight comparisons (the selector does by $0.06$--$0.10$). The limit is the learning signal (bandit feedback): the selector sees every action's outcome on every training instance.

\emph{The price of offline knowledge.} Tuning is not free. On the benchmark the validated action costs $600$ tuning runs per class, $11.4$ times the test run of the class ($25$ instances): charged to it, random choice run longer for the same total CPU time would be ahead unless the tuning uses one instance per class (which still yields $81\%$ of the gain); it pays for itself only beyond about $70$ (measured) to $130$ (interpolated) test instances per class. A Q-Learning warm-started from that one instance does not beat the static action either ($1.86$ against $1.92$ points).

\subsection{Other hosts: a flowshop, a genetic algorithm, unrelated machines and a third-party ALNS}
\label{sec:flowshop}
We repeated the protocol, in compiled code, on the permutation flowshop (nine classes, $20$ tuning and $40$ test instances each, plus the $90$ Taillard instances; $T=1000$, three seeds; five and fifteen actions), on a steady-state genetic algorithm for P$||\Cmax$ ($1{,}200$ instances, $T=10{,}000$ offspring, nine and eighteen actions) and on unrelated machines; their gains are in Table~\ref{tab:hosts}.
\emph{Flowshop.} The cross-fitted headroom is only $0.012$ and $0.025$ points. No online learner beats random choice at equal iterations (Q-Learning loses $0.05$--$0.08$, and $0.03$--$0.05$ at equal CPU time) and the validated action beats the five learners in all ten learner--space pairs of the synthetic instances ($0.020$--$0.104$).
\emph{Genetic algorithm.} The actions are complementary, so a single validated action can lose to uniform random choice ($-0.057$ with nine actions). Thompson sampling gains $0.051$ and $0.047$, Q-Learning loses $0.096$ and $0.048$, and the competitor that wins is a validated \emph{mixture}, the uniform choice among the three best single actions of each class: it gains $0.050$ and $0.144$, beats the validated single action by $0.11$ and $0.07$ and nine of the ten learner--space pairs at equal iterations and at equal CPU time.

\emph{Unrelated machines (R$||\Cmax$).} The synthetic classes sit near the ceiling, so we added a host with real headroom (twelve classes, $100$ test instances each; predictions fixed after a pilot). The one-seed headroom is $1.5$ and $2.8$ points (cross-fitted $0.43$ and $1.60$), $12$ and $23$ times that of the synthetic P$||\Cmax$, and the validated action gains $0.95$ and $2.08$.
Yet at equal iterations Q-Learning loses ($-1.06$ and $-0.48$), UCB1 and the policy gradient are at parity with random choice, and only the optimistic Q-Learning ($+0.17$, five sizes) and the ALNS roulette ($+0.15$, fifteen actions) gain beyond the margin, at most $18\%$ and $7\%$ of the validated action's gain; the validated action beats every learner in all ten learner--space pairs ($0.77$--$2.52$), also at equal CPU time ($0.94$--$2.17$), where the learners' gains come from the cost effect.
On the three published R$||\Cmax$ families (uncorrelated, correlated jobs, correlated machines; $25$ sizes, $500$ test instances per family and space) the validated action beats every learner in all $30$ learner--cell pairs ($0.42$--$2.64$) and none beats it, yet the standard Q-Learning gains beyond the margin in four of the six cells, up to $0.67$ and $0.83$ points with correlated machines ($53\%$ and $65\%$ of the validated gain): the lock-in on the first action (with the list permuted once per run it loses in all six cells, and with ties broken at random it gains in all six but at most $25\%$ of the validated gain).

\emph{A third-party host.} To rule out that our selectors or our code explain the picture, we ran the protocol unchanged on the ALNS of the \texttt{alns} library (version 7.0.0)~\cite{WoudaLan2023} for the CVRP, with its own selectors (roulette wheel, segmented roulette wheel, AlphaUCB, and a bandit selector with UCB1 or $\varepsilon$-greedy, the last two with our untuned settings) over $16$ heterogeneous destroy and repair operators, on eight synthetic classes ($20$ test instances, three seeds) and the $22$ published X instances~\cite{UchoaEtal2017} with $n\le200$.
The validated operator gains beyond the margin over random choice in $7$ of the $8$ classes ($0.6$--$1.1$ points of the cost); the best selector, the segmented roulette wheel, gains beyond the margin in $5$ classes ($0.33$--$0.52$) and the best selector of each class captures $23$--$61\%$ (mean $43\%$) of the validated gain where any selector gains; none of the five selectors beats the validated operator in any class ($0$ of $40$), the UCB1 bandit loses up to $3.9$, and permuting the operator list changes no selector.
On the X instances the mean gaps to the best known solutions are $2.51\%$ (random choice), $2.38\%$ (the operator chosen per class on the synthetic classes), $1.89\%$ (the single best operator) and $2.00\%$ (segmented roulette): online selection helps relative to random choice and never relative to the offline choice.

\section{Discussion, Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:limits}
The gains that learning-guided search reports are mostly structure (here the greedy reconstruction), budget and tuning. The learner's own contribution is nil to modest: at most $0.04$ points on the synthetic classes, about $16\%$ of a validated action's gain for a Q-Learning that does not lock in, $23$--$61\%$ for a third-party ALNS selector where any gains; no learner beat the validated action except in $2$ of $69$ cells by less than $0.02$ points.
What pays is knowledge acquired before the run: a validated action, a validated mixture where actions are complementary, or better a policy trained offline, mostly class-level knowledge. The standard Q-Learning's apparent wins (at equal CPU time, $0.15$--$0.25$ points in a published family, $53$--$65\%$ of the validated gain with correlated machines) come from repeating the action listed first.
\emph{A rule of use.} Before crediting a learner, compare it with random choice and with a validated static action or mixture at equal CPU time, pricing the tuning; report the capture and the learner's sensitivity to the order of the actions (break ties at random; permuting once per run is not enough); and check that the pipeline detects a planted effect.

\emph{Limitations.} (i) The equal-CPU comparison depends on $K$, measured on separate instances in sequence on a laptop (not always on a quiet machine) and reported for every arm. (ii) Gains are in points of the makespan, so $0.05\%$ may or may not matter, the margin is a choice, and the synthetic classes are close to the ceiling (Table~\ref{tab:classes}): the protocol compares arms, not absolute quality, and we did not compare with the solvers reviewed in~\cite{OstojicEtAl2025}; the ILS of the ablation uses one perturbation (random reinsertion) and the swap and reinsertion moves of the bottleneck machine.
(iii) Hypotheses were recorded in a dated, hash-stamped internal log under version control, not in a public registry; the batteries were sequential, so earlier results shaped later hypotheses, and the capture and correlation analyses are exploratory.
(iv) The evidence is for P$||\Cmax$, R$||\Cmax$ with three published time structures, a permutation flowshop, one genetic algorithm and one third-party ALNS with its own selectors; deep policy gradients and portfolios of hundreds of operators are outside it, and the offline selector's training cost is not charged to it.
(v) Conclusions about ``Q-Learning'' are conclusions about an implementation (initial values, tie rule): the other learners were rerun with permuted lists (no difference beyond the margin except FRRMAB). Not yet externally reviewed. \todonote{decide: advisor as coauthor; Zenodo DOI}
