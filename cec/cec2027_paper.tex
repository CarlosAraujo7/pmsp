\begin{abstract}
Learning-guided metaheuristics are routinely reported to beat non-learning counterparts, yet comparisons seldom separate what the learned policy adds from what the search structure, the CPU budget and the tuning already provide.
We propose a budget-fair attribution protocol (random control, equal CPU time, a static competitor validated offline, a cross-fitted oracle headroom, power checks, pre-recorded hypotheses) and apply it to Iterated Greedy (IG) for identical parallel machines, with a flowshop, a genetic algorithm and unrelated machines as checks.
Three findings. (1) The destruction--reconstruction mechanism, not the policy, explains the IG's advantage; the share of a local search's gain that is only budget ranges from $28\%$ to $93\%$ with the implementation.
(2) At equal iterations no online learner beats random choice by more than $0.04$ points on the synthetic classes; a validated static configuration (a single action or, where actions are complementary, a mixture) is never worse than an online learner beyond the equivalence margin on any host and at longer horizons, and irace and two published adaptive-operator-selection designs do not beat it.
(3) The apparent exceptions come from an implementation detail: the standard Q-Learning repeats the action listed first (zero-initialised table, ties broken by index), so its gains, its wins at equal CPU time and the correlation of its gain with the oracle headroom measure where a good action stood in the list; an order-invariant version gains beyond the margin in none of $69$ cells. The oracle headroom net of run-to-run noise is thus a necessary condition for Q-Learning to pay and never shown sufficient; a selector trained offline captures most of it through class-level knowledge, whereas four repairs of the online learner do not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Metaheuristics that learn while they search are a prominent line of work in evolutionary computation and operations research. A typical \emph{learnheuristic}~\cite{CalvetEtal2017} embeds a
reinforcement-learning agent, most often tabular Q-Learning~\cite{WatkinsDayan1992,SuttonBarto2018}, as an adaptive selector of operators, strategies or parameters inside a search framework such as
Iterated Local Search (ILS)~\cite{LourencaoEtal2019} or Iterated Greedy (IG)~\cite{RuizStutzle2007}, and reports that it beats the search without the agent or published baselines~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023,MissaouiOSullivan2026}.
Such results are usually read as evidence that the \emph{learned policy} is what helps.

Three confounds make that reading fragile. First, the \emph{search structure} that hosts the agent (which operators, which acceptance rule, whether there is a local search) may produce most of the gain
under any selection rule. Second, the \emph{budget}: an agent changes the CPU time per iteration and a local search multiplies it, so equal iteration counts are not equal budgets. Third, the \emph{tuning}:
a parameter validated offline on separate instances is a competitor that most studies omit. Adaptive operator selection is a well-studied topic~\cite{FialhoEtal2010,PisingerRopke2007}, but we found it rarely
answered with these controls: in an audit of $23$ recent open-access papers that embed a learned selector in a metaheuristic, $10$ include a random-selection control, $6$ compare at a common time limit,
$3$ include a static configuration tuned offline and $22$ report a benefit of the learned component ($5$ of them only partly) (a small sample, one coder; details in the repository).

We ask three questions. \textbf{RQ1:} what explains the advantage of a learning-guided IG over ILS, and how much of a local search's gain is only budget? \textbf{RQ2:} do online learners beat random choice over the same actions, at equal iterations and at equal CPU time, does a static configuration win anyway, and could learning have shown up if it existed?
\textbf{RQ3:} where, if anywhere, does an online selector pay, can that be foretold, and what exploits the headroom instead? Our contributions are
(i) a \emph{budget-fair attribution protocol} (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) with a cross-fitted oracle headroom, a power check, a positive control and a split of a learner's gain at equal CPU time into a decision effect and a cost effect, released as a small library;
(ii) evidence that the IG's advantage comes from its search structure, that the ``only budget'' share of a local search's gain depends on the implementation, and that the standard Q-Learning's wins at equal CPU time come from repeating the first, cheapest action of its list, not from learning; and
(iii) a \emph{headroom analysis}: the usual headroom estimate is mostly run-to-run noise, and with the noise removed the headroom is a necessary condition for Q-Learning to pay, though never shown sufficient; a policy learned \emph{offline} exploits it, and four repairs of the online learner do not.
Section~\ref{sec:flowshop} checks how far the findings carry on a flowshop, a genetic algorithm and unrelated machines (real headroom, threshold frozen beforehand).

\section{Related Work}
The question is close to parameter control in evolutionary algorithms~\cite{EibenEtal1999,KarafotiasEtal2015} and to selection hyper-heuristics~\cite{BurkeEtal2013,DrakeEtal2020}, which choose among low-level heuristics online;
offline configurators such as irace~\cite{LopezIbanezEtal2016} and ParamILS~\cite{HutterEtal2009} fix the parameters before the run.
Choosing among algorithms per instance is the algorithm selection problem~\cite{Rice1976}, whose libraries~\cite{BischlEtal2016} score a selector by the share of the gap between the single best solver and the per-instance oracle that it closes; our one-seed oracle is that oracle. Here the oracle of a stochastic algorithm is inflated by run-to-run noise (hence cross-fitting) and selection is learned online within a run.
Where learned neighbourhood selection beats strong baselines, the policy is trained offline~\cite{SonneratEtal2021}, as in our selector (Section~\ref{sec:selector}).

Adaptive operator selection (AOS)~\cite{FialhoEtal2010} uses probability matching~\cite{Goldberg1990}, adaptive pursuit~\cite{Thierens2005}, ALNS weights~\cite{PisingerRopke2007}, bandits~\cite{AuerEtal2002,DaCostaEtal2008}
or reinforcement learning with a state~\cite{BengioBengio2021}; the credit-assignment and selection rules of selection hyper-heuristics are the same family. Q-Learning has been used to select operators inside an IG for the permutation flowshop~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023} and, closest to this work,
the destruction size of an IG for identical machines with setups~\cite{MissaouiOSullivan2026}; both report that the learning-guided method beats its baselines. Dynamic algorithm configuration~\cite{BiedenkappEtal2020} learns such policies across instances; the pre-trained Q table below is its tabular form and the offline selector its static counterpart.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Problem, instances and bounds}
In P$||\Cmax$, $n$ jobs with times $p_j$ are assigned to $m$ identical machines to minimise $\Cmax=\max_i\sum_{j\in S_i}p_j$.
The classical bound is $\mathrm{LB}=\max(\max_jp_j,\lceil\sum_jp_j/m\rceil)$~\cite{Graham1969}, and $\mathrm{GAP}=100(\Cmax-\mathrm{LB})/\mathrm{LB}$. A tighter, certified bound comes from the linear relaxation of the arc-flow
model~\cite{MradSouayah2018}; it equals the best makespan found in $75.3\%$ of the synthetic instances (proving optimality). The synthetic instances have $p_j\sim U[1,100]$ in twelve classes
$(n,m)$ from $(20,4)$ to $(300,20)$; each class has $100$ test instances and $30$ separate tuning instances. With integer times one unit of makespan is $0.12$--$0.78\%$ of it (median $0.38\%$), so a mean difference of
$0.05\%$ averages many ties ($31$--$46\%$ of the instance pairs tie after averaging five seeds). The published families B, C, E and F of the benchmark reviewed in~\cite{OstojicEtAl2025} give disjoint sets: $525$ tuning instances, $2{,}550$ \emph{discovery} and $3{,}950$ \emph{held-out}
test instances (families B, C, E), the $5{,}720$ B, C, E instances on which an offline selector is trained, and $780$ of family F (never used before); instances with times near $10^{18}$ do not fit the $64$-bit compiled core and were excluded ($378$ of the $7{,}403$ of the first three sets and $280$ of the $6{,}000$ training ones, all in family B).

\subsection{Search mechanisms and learners}
\label{sec:learners}
The \textbf{IG} starts from LPT (or from the best of LPT, MULTIFIT and LDM) and alternates destruction (removing $d$ jobs) and greedy reconstruction (non-increasing time onto the least-loaded machine), with greedy
acceptance $\Cmax(x')\leq\Cmax(x)$ and no separate perturbation phase. The \textbf{ILS} starts from LPT and, at each iteration, a selector chooses one of four neighbourhood operators (inter-machine swap,
reinsertion, intra-machine swap, block move) under best improvement; after $50$ iterations without improvement it restarts from the best solution. The \emph{actions} are the destruction strategy (random, longest jobs, machine-concentrated; $3$ actions), the destruction size
($d/n\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20,0.30,0.40\}$; $5$ actions), their $15$ combinations, these $15$ with or without a descent after reconstruction ($30$ actions), or the four ILS operators.
The reward of an iteration is the improvement of the makespan, truncated at zero. At each iteration a selector $\pi$ chooses an action from the state of the current solution (four features: gap to the bound, load imbalance, fraction of the budget used, stagnation), the IG destroys, reconstructs and optionally descends accordingly, $\pi$ receives the reward and the candidate is accepted if it does not worsen the makespan; the selector is the only component that changes between arms.
Two further hosts use the same selectors and protocol, in compiled code: the \textbf{permutation flowshop} with the IG of Ruiz and St\"utzle (NEH start, insertion local search, constant-temperature acceptance; the actions are the destruction size, alone or crossed with three temperature factors) a \textbf{steady-state genetic algorithm} for P$||\Cmax$ (population $20$, binary tournament, the child replaces the worst individual; an action is a crossover crossed with a mutation and one of two intensities) and the IG for \textbf{unrelated machines} (R$||\Cmax$; times $p_{jk}\sim U[1,100]$ drawn per machine, reconstruction by earliest completion).

The \emph{learners} are: tabular Q-Learning over $81$ states ($\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.9$,
$\varepsilon$-greedy from $1.0$ to $0.05$ by $0.995$ per step)~\cite{WatkinsDayan1992}; UCB1~\cite{AuerEtal2002}; a policy-gradient learner (softmax preferences per state, learning rate chosen on tuning instances)~\cite{Williams1992,SuttonBarto2018}; six methods without state, all tuned on the tuning instances: probability matching, adaptive pursuit, an ALNS-style roulette, Thompson sampling with discounting~\cite{Russo2018}, DMAB~\cite{DaCostaEtal2008} and an online racing learner; LinUCB on a continuous context~\cite{LiEtAl2010};
and a DQN with two layers of $64$ units, replay and a target network~\cite{MnihEtAl2015}. Q-Learning itself is tuned per family only in Section~\ref{sec:selector}. The static competitor is the best fixed action per class,
chosen on the tuning instances.

\subsection{The budget-fair attribution protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
\emph{Terms.} A \emph{class} is an $(n,m)$ pair, a published family by size tercile or, in family F, a subset with $m$ and $n$; a \emph{cell} is a class in one action space (five or fifteen actions) and is the unit of the headroom analysis; the \emph{validated action} is the best fixed action per class, chosen on separate instances; the \emph{headroom} of a cell is the gain over random choice of the best fixed action per instance. ``Level'' means parity or no significant difference; \emph{registered} results were specified before the battery was run, \emph{post hoc} ones were not.
(1)~\emph{Random control at a matched budget:} the effect of the policy is the difference between the learner and uniform random choice over the same actions, iterations and instances.
(2)~\emph{Equal CPU time, calibrated sequentially:} for each pair, the CPU time ratio $K=\mathrm{CPU}_1/\mathrm{CPU}_2$ of $1000$ iterations of the first arm to $1000$ of the second is measured on separate instances, in sequence, and the second arm receives $\mathrm{round}(1000K)$ iterations; as $K$ is a property of the implementation, every arm must be implemented with comparable efficiency, and $K$ is reported. A learner's gain at equal CPU time is the sum of a
\emph{decision effect}, its gain at equal iterations, and a \emph{cost effect}, the extra iterations the random control loses by matching the CPU time of a learner that chooses cheaper actions: $G_{\mathrm{CPU}}=G_{\mathrm{dec}}+G_{\mathrm{cost}}$ with
$G_{\mathrm{dec}}=-\bar d_{\mathrm{iter}}$ and $G_{\mathrm{cost}}=\bar d_{\mathrm{iter}}-\bar d_{\mathrm{CPU}}$.
(3)~\emph{A static competitor validated offline} on separate instances is what a practitioner would use: the best fixed action per class, and, when actions are complementary, a uniform mixture of the best few. (4)~\emph{Cross-fitted oracle headroom:} the gain over random
choice of the best fixed action per instance estimates what a selector that picks one action per instance could gain; the action is chosen on two seeds and evaluated on two fresh ones, because the best of one seed per action carries run-to-run noise (Section~\ref{sec:published}). (5)~\emph{Power and a positive control:} the minimum detectable gain is estimated by planting gains in the paired differences,
and the learners and the pipeline are checked on a planted reward bonus and on a static policy chosen with class information. (6)~\emph{Statistics specified beforehand:} per instance (the mean of the seeds), $d=100(A-B)/B$ in the makespan is tested in pairs
(Wilcoxon, two-sided, ties dropped), with Holm correction over each family, a bootstrap $90\%$ interval, and an equivalence margin~\cite{Lakens2017} of $\pm0.05\%$, about an eighth of one makespan unit on the median synthetic instance; a Holm-significant difference whose interval lies inside
the margin is reported as parity, and one whose interval crosses the margin as a small win. Throughout the text and tables a \emph{gain} is $-\bar d$ in percentage points of the makespan (written ``points'' or $\%$), positive when the first-named arm is better. Hypotheses, decision rules and predictions were recorded, with md5 hashes of the code, in a dated diary before each battery was run.

\subsection{Implementations}
The local search was first written with Python loops, then vectorized ($120$ of $120$ runs identical), then compiled with the IG, the destruction strategies and the agents (numba), exact on the deterministic parts and statistically equivalent on the random draws
(inside $\pm0.05\%$ in five checks); it also counts evaluations. The other hosts have their own compiled cores with the same agent code ($1{,}561$ unit tests in all). All runs used one laptop (Intel Core i5-12450H, 8 cores, 16 GB), where $K$ was measured.

\subsection{RQ1: the search structure and the budget, not the policy}
\label{sec:rq1}
\emph{Structure.} Against LPT and seven ILS variants in the twelve classes, the Q-Learning-guided IG is significantly better, after Holm correction, in $72$ of $96$ comparisons (Python study, equal iterations) and never worse. Yet its advantage over its four ablation siblings
(random selection, UCB1 with and without state, Q-Learning without state) is negligible: $0.011\%$ ($90\%$ interval $0.003$ to $0.019$; ten algorithm seeds on $240$ synthetic instances). The mechanism is large: with learning removed from both sides, the IG beats the ILS at equal iterations (Python implementation) in $10$ of $12$ classes (the other two differ by less than $0.01\%$) by $0.49\%$, against $0.53\%$ for the learned versions.
This does not depend on how efficiently the ILS is implemented: with vectorized operators ($6.2$ times faster; an iteration costing $0.4$ to $1.9$ times that of the IG), the IG still beats the ILS by $0.46\%$ at equal CPU time ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-80}$), with a positive gain in all twelve classes.

\emph{Budget.} Adding a short best-improvement descent after each reconstruction improves the IG by $0.150\%$ at an equal number of iterations, but it costs CPU time, so the verdict at equal CPU depends on the cost ratio $K$, and $K$ depends on the implementation.
With the first, loop-based descent ($K=9.6$) giving the plain IG the CPU time of the hybrid leaves an advantage of only $0.010\%$: $93\%$ of the gain would have been budget. Vectorizing the descent cuts $K$ to $1.8$ and the advantage at equal CPU is $0.105\%$
($30\%$ budget); compiling both arms gives $K=2.0$ and $0.115\%$ of the $0.159\%$ at equal iterations ($28\%$), a reading registered before the compiled run. For the practical recipe (tuned $d$, start from the best constructive solution) the descent is neutral at equal CPU time ($-0.008\%$ and $-0.006\%$, inside the margin). An evaluation count agrees in sign but not in size ($0.080\%$, $50\%$ budget); equal CPU time remains the budget of choice.

\subsection{RQ2: do learners beat random choice, does a static configuration win, and could learning have shown up?}
\label{sec:rq2}
Compiled runs are about a hundred times faster, which allows five seeds per instance in four IG action spaces
(Table~\ref{tab:agents}). $K$ in Table~\ref{tab:agents} is the cost of a whole run: $1.01$--$1.08$ for UCB1 and the policy gradient, $1.07$ for Q-Learning with three strategies and $0.50$--$0.60$ in the three larger spaces, because it repeats the action listed first, the smallest and cheapest (Section~\ref{sec:published}). \emph{At an equal number of iterations none of the three original learners
gains more than $0.016\%$ over random choice in any of the $12$ comparisons}, within the equivalence margin (means over the $12$ classes, Fig.~\ref{fig:class}).
\emph{At equal CPU time Q-Learning does win} in the two spaces of sizes, by $0.059\%$ and $0.049\%$, \emph{but only by choosing cheaper actions}: its decision effect is nil ($-0.003$ and $-0.004$ points) and its cost effect is $0.062$ and $0.053$ (an effect of the order of the actions, not of learning: Section~\ref{sec:published}). Among the classic AOS methods only the ALNS-style roulette gains: $0.039$ points at equal iterations with five sizes
(inside the margin; $0.068$ at equal CPU time, outside it) and nothing at equal iterations with fifteen actions ($0.020$ at equal CPU time); the others, racing aside, stay within $0.013$ points, and online racing, the within-run analogue of a racing configurator, is \emph{worse} than random choice ($-0.012$ and $-0.067$ points).
The configuration validated per class is ahead of every one of the nine methods by $0.053$--$0.147$ points in the spaces of $5$ and $15$ actions ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}<10^{-57}$)
and level with the three original learners in the other two, where the oracle headroom is smaller ($0.016$ points behind Q-Learning with three strategies). Where the actions differ in effect and cost (the four ILS operators, $1{,}200$ instances) a DQN and LinUCB lose to the uniform mixture ($0.041\%$ and $0.066\%$), as does the operator validated per class ($0.062\%$).

\emph{Could learning have shown up?}\label{sec:longnote} \emph{Power.} With $1{,}200$ pairs the analytic minimum detectable gain at $80\%$ power is $0.006$--$0.014$ points; planting gains in the paired differences gives, in the ten comparisons with a negligible observed effect, $58$--$99.8\%$ power at $0.02$ points and at least $97\%$ at $0.03$,
while the largest gain of Q-Learning, UCB1 or the policy gradient is $0.016$: gains of $0.03$ points or more are excluded for them. \emph{Positive controls.} The per-class validated action is detected at $0.092$ and $0.080$ points, and with a reward bonus planted on one action
all six learners tested choose it at least $1.5$ times more often than uniform.

\emph{Horizon and finer times.} With five sizes and $T$ from $5{,}000$ to $100{,}000$ the learners stay within $0.006$ points of random choice while the one-seed oracle headroom falls from $0.125\%$ to $0.015\%$, where the validated action still leads them by $0.012$--$0.017$ points ($p<10^{-12}$). On unrelated machines ($T$ up to $100{,}000$) the validated gain falls from $1.00$ and $2.18$ to $0.31$ and $0.45$ points, Q-Learning never beats random choice ($-0.13$ to $-0.95$) and the validated action beats every learner at every horizon ($0.37$--$2.33$). With finer times ($p_j\sim U[1,10^4]$) no learner gains more than $0.015$ points.

\emph{Time-varying and state-dependent competitors} (Table~\ref{tab:further}). The best three-phase schedule of the five sizes per class gains $0.095$ against $0.092$ for the validated action on P$||\Cmax$ (parity) and loses $0.166$ to it on unrelated machines; a myopic policy and a rollout policy built on the validated action get hundreds of times a learner's evaluations, and the validated action beats both in all eight cells ($0.04$--$1.58$ points); they are references, not upper bounds.

\emph{A configurator} (Table~\ref{tab:further}). irace~\cite{LopezIbanezEtal2016}, given the tuning cost of the validated action, tunes a continuous destruction size (and strategy) per class: parity with the validated action on P$||\Cmax$ (within $0.012$ points), $0.15$ and $0.19$ points behind on R$||\Cmax$, and still never ahead beyond the margin with ten times the budget. Tuning Q-Learning's four hyperparameters (learning rate, discount, exploration decay and floor) per class selects high exploration floors (median $0.26$--$0.37$) and beats the default Q-Learning on R$||\Cmax$ by $0.58$ and $0.63$ points (paired, on its own test instances), yet the validated action beats it in all four host--space pairs ($0.06$--$1.90$ points).

\subsection{RQ3: where does an online selector pay, can that be foretold, and what exploits the headroom?}
\label{sec:published}\label{sec:selector}
\emph{The oracle headroom is mostly noise unless it is cross-fitted.} Taken as the best of one seed per action, the headroom of the synthetic classes is $0.125$ and $0.122$ points (five and fifteen actions); choosing the action on two seeds and evaluating it on two fresh ones removes half to two thirds of it ($0.063$ and $0.043$), and $28$--$40\%$ of the headroom of the published instances (only $15$--$20\%$ in family C, middle and upper terciles).
The cross-fitted value is a lower bound, the one-seed value an upper bound.

\emph{Published instances.} We ran the nine learners on $2{,}550$ of the published instances, with the hyperparameters and the validated action fixed per family and size tercile on $525$ separate ones. \emph{Here the standard Q-Learning seemed to beat random choice} (a reading withdrawn below):
Q-Learning gains $0.058$ points at equal iterations with fifteen actions and the ALNS roulette $0.057$ with five sizes; in family C, middle and upper size terciles, Q-Learning gains $0.16$ and $0.24$ points, while in family E it \emph{loses} $0.06$--$0.07$.
We fixed this reading as hypotheses before running $3{,}950$ held-out instances, retuning nothing. All five confirmatory tests held : Q-Learning gains $0.245$ and $0.149$ points in family C, middle and upper terciles, with fifteen and five actions, and loses $0.052$ and $0.059$ in family E; the ALNS roulette gains $0.056$ with five sizes. At equal CPU time the validated action is Holm-significantly ahead of the learner in all $54$ learner--space--set pairs, though only level with the ALNS roulette with five sizes ($0.023$ and $0.024$).

\emph{A necessary condition, not a sufficient one.} Across the cells of a space, the rank correlation between the cross-fitted headroom and Q-Learning's gain is $0.95$ and $0.97$ on the held-out instances (against $0.67$ and $0.60$ with the biased headroom), $0.64$ and $0.66$ across the twelve synthetic classes, and $-0.27$ and $-0.07$ on the flowshop (against $-0.93$ and $-0.78$, a correlation that was mostly noise). We adopted the cross-fitted estimator after the biased one failed in the synthetic classes and the flowshop. These are correlations with the gain of the \emph{standard} Q-Learning; they do not survive the check below.

\emph{One threshold (standard Q-Learning).} A single threshold, $h^*=0.048$ points, chosen on the $18$ discovery cells and applied unchanged to the $60$ others, classified $78\%$ of them correctly for the standard learner (balanced accuracy $0.80$, $95\%$ interval $0.67$--$0.91$; Fig.~\ref{fig:class}), an excess over always predicting ``no gain'' ($68\%$) that is not significant. Post hoc, in the $150$ cells outside the discovery set: below $h^*$ there are $71$ cells and in none does Q-Learning gain beyond the margin; above it the standard learner does in $29$ of $79$.

\emph{An implementation artefact: the order of the actions.} The standard Q-Learning (zero-initialised table, non-negative reward, ties broken by the first index) repeats the action listed first: in a diagnostic on a further benchmark (the $R||\Cmax$ instances of Fanjul-Peyro and Ruiz, in the repository) it puts $55$--$73\%$ of its choices on it, UCB1, the policy gradient and the ALNS roulette stay at the share of random choice, and with the validated action moved to the first position it recovers $81$--$86\%$ of the validated gain. Our lists run from the smallest to the largest destruction, so the first action is the cheapest, which explains the wins at equal CPU time on P$||\Cmax$. With the actions permuted at random in each run, Q-Learning gains beyond the margin in none of $69$ cells ($24$ synthetic and $18$ held-out published P$||\Cmax$ cells, $6$ published R$||\Cmax$ cells, $21$ cells of the standard benchmark), evaluates $0.99$--$1.01$ times as many solutions as random choice, and on the held-out published instances \emph{loses} $0.25$ and $0.12$ points in family C, middle and upper terciles, where the standard learner gained $0.245$ and $0.149$. The rank correlation between the headroom and its gain on the $18$ held-out cells, $+0.65$ for the standard learner, is $-0.74$. The positive half of the rule (Q-Learning pays where the headroom is large) is therefore an artefact; the negative half stands because the order-invariant learner pays nowhere.
\emph{What exploits the headroom: a policy learned offline.} We trained an ExtraTrees regressor on $5{,}720$ B, C, E instances disjoint from every tuning and test set, with scale-free features of the instance ($n$, $m$, dispersion and quantiles of the times, bound ratios, the gap of LPT; no family label), to predict the relative performance of each fixed action; at test time it picks one action per instance.
The model and the hypotheses were fixed before any test run. On the discovery and held-out instances (Table~\ref{tab:selector}) it gains $0.024$--$0.044$ points over the validated action (inside the margin, hence parity, except in the larger space on held-out instances, where the $90\%$ interval crosses the margin), $0.08$--$0.095$ over Q-Learning and $0.04$--$0.14$ over the ALNS roulette at equal iterations, and captures $0.79$--$0.84$ of the one-seed oracle headroom (an upper bound) against $0.58$--$0.66$ for the validated action. A control run afterwards qualifies the result: a per-class lookup table learned on the same instances is level with the selector on the held-out instances, where every class was seen in training ($0.004$ and $0.006$ points, not significant), and the selector wins by only $0.017$ and $0.014$ on the discovery instances ($59\%$ of whose classes were seen): most of the gain is class-level knowledge.

\emph{Repairing the online learner.} Four attempts do not change the order. Tuning Q-Learning per family adds $0.009$--$0.014$ points; rewarding plateau moves, as the ALNS score does, adds $0.015$ with five sizes and \emph{costs} $0.007$--$0.009$ with fifteen; the validated action beats both in all eight comparisons, and the selector beats them by $0.06$--$0.10$. A Q table \emph{pre-trained} offline on the same instances is no better than a cold one with five sizes
($-0.006$ and $+0.005$ points) and much worse with fifteen ($-0.146$ and $-0.143$). Optimistic initialization stays level with random choice ($0.004$--$0.008$ points), is worse than the cold Q-Learning ($0.007$--$0.051$) and loses to the validated action by $0.067$--$0.104$. The limit is the learning signal (bandit feedback): the selector sees every action's outcome on every training instance.

\subsection{Other hosts: a flowshop, a genetic algorithm and unrelated machines}
\label{sec:flowshop}
We repeated the protocol, in compiled code, on the permutation flowshop (nine classes, $n\in\{20,50,100\}$, $m\in\{5,10,20\}$, with $20$ tuning and $40$ test instances each, plus the $90$ instances ta001--ta090 of Taillard; $T=1000$, three seeds; five and fifteen actions), on a steady-state genetic algorithm for P$||\Cmax$ ($1{,}200$ instances, $T=10{,}000$ offspring, nine and eighteen actions) and on unrelated machines; their gains are in Table~\ref{tab:hosts} and their cells (except the last host's) in Fig.~\ref{fig:class}.
\emph{Flowshop.} The cross-fitted headroom is only $0.012$ and $0.025$ points. No online learner beats random choice at equal iterations (Q-Learning loses $0.05$--$0.08$ points, and $0.03$--$0.05$ at equal CPU time) and the validated action beats the five learners (Q-Learning, UCB1, the policy gradient, the ALNS roulette, Thompson sampling) in all ten learner--space pairs of the synthetic instances at equal iterations (by $0.020$--$0.104$).

\emph{Genetic algorithm.} The actions are complementary, so a single validated action can lose to uniform random choice ($-0.057$ points with nine actions). Thompson sampling gains $0.051$ and $0.047$ points, Q-Learning loses $0.096$ and $0.048$, and the competitor that wins is a validated \emph{mixture}, the uniform choice among the three best single actions of each class: it gains $0.050$ and $0.144$ points, beats the validated single action by $0.105$ and $0.067$ and nine of the ten learner--space pairs at equal iterations and at equal CPU time.

\emph{Unrelated machines (R$||\Cmax$).} The synthetic classes sit near the ceiling, so we added a host with real headroom, tested with the threshold frozen and the hypotheses registered beforehand (the same twelve classes, $100$ test instances each, $p_{jk}\sim U[1,100]$; predictions fixed after a pilot). The one-seed headroom is $1.5$ and $2.8$ points (cross-fitted $0.43$ and $1.60$), $12$ and $23$ times that of the synthetic P$||\Cmax$ ($7$ and $37$ times cross-fitted), and the validated action gains $0.95$ and $2.08$ points. Yet at equal iterations Q-Learning loses ($-1.06$ and $-0.48$), UCB1 and the policy gradient are at parity with random choice, and, in the main battery, only the optimistic Q-Learning ($+0.17$, five sizes) and the ALNS roulette ($+0.15$, fifteen actions) gain beyond the margin, at most $18\%$ and $7\%$ of the validated action's gain; the validated action beats every learner in all ten learner--space pairs, by $0.77$--$2.52$ points.

\emph{At equal CPU time} Q-Learning with fifteen actions and the ALNS roulette gain over random choice ($0.58$ and $0.39$--$0.45$ points) through the cost effect (decision effects $-0.48$ and $-0.09$ to $+0.15$), and the validated action, with its own cost ratio, still beats all five learners in all ten pairs ($0.94$--$2.17$).

\emph{An offline selector} trained on $1{,}200$ further instances ($18{,}000$ and $54{,}000$ training runs, five times the tuning of the validated action) beats the best online learner of the main battery by $0.72$ and $1.91$ points but captures $60\%$ and $73\%$ of the headroom, no more than the validated action ($-0.056$ and $-0.023$ points; level with the class table). \emph{Two published AOS designs}, tuned per space on the tuning instances as their hyperparameters were not available (an episodic Q-Learning with a binary state in the style of~\cite{KarimiMamaghanEtal2023} and an FRRMAB-style sliding-window bandit~\cite{LiFialhoEtal2014}), do not close the distance (Table~\ref{tab:further}): neither gains beyond the margin on P$||\Cmax$, and on R$||\Cmax$ they reach at most $14\%$ of the validated action's gain, which beats both in all eight comparisons ($0.06$--$1.82$ points). The frozen rule is not a classifier here ($42\%$ of the $24$ cells).

\emph{Published families.} On the three published R$||\Cmax$ families (uncorrelated, correlated jobs, correlated machines; $25$ sizes, $500$ test instances per family and space; only the validated action chosen anew) the validated action beats every learner in all $30$ learner--cell pairs ($0.42$--$2.64$ points) and none beats it, yet the standard Q-Learning gains beyond the margin in four of the six cells, up to $0.67$ and $0.83$ points with correlated machines ($53\%$ and $65\%$ of the validated gain), a reading we withdraw: with the actions permuted at random it loses in all six cells (from $-0.05$ to $-0.66$ points). There irace's static configuration is at parity with the validated action in every cell (within $0.07$ points), irace-tuned Q-Learning (same tie rule) gains beyond the margin in five cells (up to $0.82$ points) and the two published designs up to $0.54$; the validated action beats these three in all $18$ comparisons ($0.40$--$2.19$ points).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The gains that learning-guided search reports are mostly structure (destruction--reconstruction), budget and tuning. A learner's own contribution, the decision effect, is bounded by one quantity, the headroom of the best fixed action net of run-to-run noise: below about $0.05$ points the online learner never gains beyond the margin, above it the standard Q-Learning sometimes recovers part of what a validated action gives and often loses, and even where the headroom is large (unrelated machines) a learner captures a small fraction with uniform times. The apparent exceptions (up to two thirds of the validated gain with correlated machines; gains in a published family) disappear when the order of the actions is randomised: the standard learner repeats the action listed first (none of $69$ cells with an order-invariant Q-Learning). What headroom rewards is knowledge acquired before the run (a validated action, or a selector trained offline, which captures about four fifths of the one-seed oracle gain on P$||\Cmax$ and $60$--$73\%$ on unrelated machines, mostly class-level knowledge); where the actions are complementary (a genetic algorithm, the ILS operators) the static competitor must be a \emph{mixture}.
\emph{A rule of use.} Estimate the cross-fitted headroom on tuning instances (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) or its cheap proxy, the validated-gain-to-noise ratio: it bounds what a validated action or an offline policy can capture, not what an online learner will find (its apparent power to predict where the standard Q-Learning gains, AUC $0.75$ and $0.73$ on the $26$ classes of an unseen family, reflects that learner's lock-in). Below about $0.05$ points no online learner paid, and in all cases a validated action or mixture, or better a policy trained offline, is the baseline to beat; report the learner's order sensitivity (permute the actions or break ties at random).

\emph{What the registered predictions showed.} The batteries were specified before they were run; $244$ of $308$ point predictions held in $40$ batteries (and $23$ of $29$ in the five batteries of the order check, Entries 258, 260, 261, 264). The misses (cost ratios, the classic AOS methods, the flowshop, finer times) led to the headroom rule and to cross-fitting. The headline pairs keep their significance under Pratt's treatment of ties and a permutation test, and $663$ of the $685$ confirmed tests survive a global Benjamini--Yekutieli control over the $1{,}169$ tests of all registered batteries.

\emph{Limitations.} (i) The equal-CPU comparison depends on $K$, measured on separate instances in sequence (not always on a quiet machine) and reported for every arm. (ii) Gains are in points of the makespan, so $0.05\%$ may or may not matter, and the margin is a choice; the IG with random choice reaches the classical lower bound in $42$--$48\%$ of the synthetic runs, and we did not compare it with the solvers reviewed in~\cite{OstojicEtAl2025}: the protocol compares arms, not absolute quality.
(iii) Each battery has its own Holm family and registration; the headroom rule began as an exploratory reading and rests on few cells per space and host (its threshold was chosen on the discovery cells, and its other readings were post hoc). (iv) The evidence is for P$||\Cmax$, R$||\Cmax$ with three published time structures, the permutation flowshop and one genetic algorithm.
(v) Conclusions about ``Q-Learning'' are conclusions about an implementation (initial values, tie rule): only the tabular learner was rerun with permuted actions, and the irace-tuned one keeps its tie rule. Deep policy gradients and portfolios of hundreds of operators are outside the evidence, horizons up to $T=10^5$ were studied on two hosts, the cross-fitted headroom is a conservative, downward-biased estimate, and the offline selector's training cost is not charged to it ($85{,}800$ and $257{,}400$ IG runs, about $0.7$ CPU-hours). (vi) The pre-specification is a dated, hash-stamped diary under version control, not a public registry.

\section{Conclusion}
A learned operator selector is not what explains the advantage of an IG over ILS for identical parallel machines: that belongs to destruction--reconstruction, and the share of a local search's gain that is only budget ($93\%$, $30\%$ or $28\%$ by implementation) makes the CPU cost ratio a result to report.
The oracle headroom net of run-to-run noise is a necessary condition for Q-Learning to pay and was never shown sufficient: no cell below about $0.05$ points gains beyond the margin, and the gains of the standard Q-Learning above it ($0.15$--$0.25$ points in the larger classes of a published family, wins at equal CPU time, $53$--$65\%$ of the validated gain with correlated machines) come from its repeating the action listed first; an order-invariant version gains beyond the margin in none of $69$ cells. A static configuration was never worse than an online learner beyond the margin, even on unrelated machines, and a selector trained offline exploits the headroom, mostly through class-level knowledge; four repairs of the online learner do not help.
