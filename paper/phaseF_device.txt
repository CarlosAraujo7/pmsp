Learnheuristics with Adaptive Operator and Strategy Selection for
the Parallel Machine Scheduling Problem: Hybridizing Iterated
Local Search and Iterated Greedy with Online Q-Learning
Carlos Vinicio Dantas Araújo∗
Graduate Program in Computer Science, State University of Ceará (UECE), Fortaleza, CE, Brazil

Abstract
We propose two learnheuristics that embed online tabular Q-Learning as a context-sensitive
Adaptive Operator/Strategy Selection (AOS) mechanism for the Parallel Machine Scheduling
Problem (P||Cmax ), minimising the makespan. The first, ig_ql, uses Q-Learning to select
among three destruction strategies within an Iterated Greedy (IG) destruction-reconstruction
cycle. The second, ils_ql, uses Q-Learning to select among four neighbourhood operators
within an Iterated Local Search (ILS) framework; both share a compact 81-state representation of search context. Across six synthetic instance classes (20–100 jobs, 4–20 machines, 30
instances each) and a further validation on over 1 500 published benchmark instances, ig_ql
significantly outperforms three state-of-the-art baselines (MULTIFIT, a simulated-annealing
method, and a Bee Colony Optimisation algorithm) with Holm-Bonferroni-corrected significance and non-negligible effect sizes; a four-way ablation study shows the gain requires both
the IG search mechanism and Q-Learning’s state-conditioned selection, with neither alone
suﬀicient, and the result is confirmed at N = 100 instances per class. In contrast, ils_ql
performs statistically equivalently to random operator selection (Wilcoxon p > 0.05); its apparently interpretable learned policy – a near-constant preference for one operator – is shown
to be substantially an artefact of sparse state coverage and initialisation tie-breaking rather
than a purely structural property of the problem, a methodological caution for AOS research
based on tabular reinforcement learning. An isolation control further shows that, for ils_ql,
extended computational budget contributes more than AOS sophistication.

1. Introduction
Scheduling problems are among the most studied in combinatorial optimisation, with
applications in manufacturing, cloud computing, task allocation in distributed systems, and
logistics (Pinedo, 2016). The Parallel Machine Scheduling Problem (PMSP) consists of assigning n jobs to m identical parallel machines so as to minimise the makespan Cmax , defined
as the completion time of the last job. Even for m = 2 the problem is NP-hard (Garey
and Johnson, 1979), which makes exact methods impractical for medium- and large-sized
instances.
Metaheuristics are widely adopted for the PMSP. Iterated Local Search (ILS) (Lourenço
et al., 2019) is one of the most popular approaches, alternating between a local-improvement
phase and a perturbation step to escape local optima. Iterated Greedy (IG) (Ruiz and Stützle,
2007) is a closely related framework that instead alternates cycles of destruction (removing
a subset of jobs from the current sequence) and reconstruction (greedily reinserting them);
the destruction-reconstruction cycle itself provides both search and diversification, with no
separate perturbation phase. In both frameworks, when multiple neighbourhood operators
or destruction strategies are available, the question of which one to apply at each iteration
arises: Adaptive Operator Selection (AOS) addresses this demand through mechanisms that
estimate or learn the relative effectiveness of each choice during the search (Fialho et al.,
2010; Karimi-Mamaghan et al., 2022).
Classical AOS strategies include uniform random selection (Pisinger and Ropke, 2007),
bandit algorithms such as UCB (Upper Confidence Bound) (Auer et al., 2002), and datadriven predictors such as Random Forests (RF) (Maturana and Saubion, 2008). A limitation
of these approaches is that they generally do not exploit the context of the current search:
solution quality, degree of load imbalance, budget progress, and stagnation level.
Reinforcement Learning (RL), and Q-Learning in particular (Watkins and Dayan, 1992;
Sutton and Barto, 2018), offers a context-sensitive AOS mechanism: the agent maintains
action-state value estimates and updates its selection policy based on the return obtained at
2

each iteration. Hybrid RL-metaheuristic approaches have shown promise in vehicle routing
(Zhang et al., 2020) and job-shop scheduling (Park et al., 2021). In the scheduling literature, Q-Learning as an AOS mechanism has been explored mainly for permutation flowshop,
integrated with iterated greedy (Karimi-Mamaghan et al., 2023) and with dynamic operatorportfolio management schemes (Karimi-Mamaghan et al., 2024); for the PMSP specifically,
RL has already been combined with swarm-intelligence metaheuristics in variants with setup
and release times, but for dynamic parameter tuning rather than operator selection (Ozsoydan, 2025). More recently, Q-Learning has been integrated with Iterated Greedy for
the PMSP with sequence-dependent setup times, targeting weighted earliness/tardiness –
not makespan – via greedy destruction-reconstruction rather than neighbourhood operators (Missaoui and O’Sullivan, 2026b). The use of tabular Q-Learning as AOS over neighbourhood operators within an ILS, specifically for the pure PMSP (P||Cmax, identical machines, makespan minimisation), remains, to our knowledge, unexplored. Our second learnheuristic, ig_ql, employs tabular Q-Learning analogously within an Iterated Greedy scheme
(destruction-reconstruction), in the same general spirit as (Missaoui and O’Sullivan, 2026b);
the difference lies both in the problem variant/objective and in the learning mechanism.
Regarding the variant: while Missaoui and O’Sullivan (2026b) address sequence-dependent
setup times, due-date windows, and weighted earliness/tardiness minimisation, our ig_ql
addresses the pure PMSP (P||Cmax, identical machines, no setups) with makespan minimisation – the classical variant of the problem. Regarding the mechanism, full-text access to
(Missaoui and O’Sullivan, 2026b) confirms substantial differences across every component
of the agent: there, Q-Learning selects the destruction size d (five discrete numeric values,
d ∈ {1, . . . , 5}) for a single, fixed “strategic” destruction-reconstruction procedure (insertion
at the first feasible position, advancing on deterioration, a technique from (Missaoui and
Ruiz, 2022)), over a state space the authors describe as the solutions themselves (with no
search-feature abstraction), with a reward given by (fbefore − fafter )/10 on improvement, 1
when the objective value is unchanged, and 0 on deterioration, and simulated-annealing-style
acceptance (a temperature parameter). In contrast, our ig_ql uses Q-Learning to select
among three qualitatively distinct destruction strategies (random removal, removal of the
longest-processing-time jobs, and machine-concentrated removal), keeping the destruction

This article makes the following five contributions. (i) ig_ql, a learnheuristic that embeds tabular Q-Learning as an online destruction-strategy-selection mechanism within an
Iterated Greedy framework for the PMSP; across six synthetic instance classes and a further
validation on over 1 500 published benchmark instances (Section 5), it significantly outperforms three state-of-the-art baselines – MULTIFIT, a simulated-annealing method following
(Li and Perkins, 2024), and a Bee Colony Optimisation algorithm (Davidović et al., 2012) –
with Holm-Bonferroni-corrected significance and non-negligible effect sizes. (ii) A four-way
ablation study isolating the IG search mechanism from Q-Learning, and state-conditioning
from temporal-difference learning, which identifies which components of ig_ql drive its advantage, together with a replication at N = 100 instances per class confirming the result is
not a small-sample artefact. (iii) ils_ql, a companion learnheuristic embedding the same
tabular Q-Learning mechanism as an Adaptive Operator Selection module within Iterated
Local Search, with a compact 81-state representation of search context, evaluated across
eight AOS configurations on 30 distinct instances per class; we show that the effectively
reachable coverage of this state space, within a single run’s budget, is substantially smaller
than nominal (Section 5.9.3), a finding relevant to state-space design for online AOS. (iv)
An isolation-control experiment (ILS-Rand-HI vs. ILS-QL-HI) that separates the effect of extended computational budget from that of online learning, a methodological step rarely taken
in the AOS literature. (v) An interpretability analysis of the learned Q-table policy, including a diagnostic of how table initialisation and sparse state coverage can artificially inflate
the appearance of a near-constant policy – a methodological caution relevant to RL-based
AOS research.

2. Related Work
2.1. Adaptive Operator Selection
AOS has emerged as a well-established research area within evolutionary algorithms and
hyper-heuristics (Burke et al., 2013). Fialho et al. (2010) conducted a comprehensive survey of bandit-based AOS mechanisms applied to genetic algorithms, finding that simple
exploration-exploitation strategies frequently outperform more complex methods in small
operator spaces. Maturana and Saubion (2008) proposed a Random-Forest-based selector
that learns to map search states to operators via supervised regression, obtaining gains on
constraint satisfaction problems. Pisinger and Ropke (2007) introduced ALNS (Adaptive
Large Neighbourhood Search), in which operator weights are updated based on recent performance, obtaining benchmark results on routing problems.
Despite these advances, most AOS methods do not explicitly model the state of the search:
the selected operator does not depend on features such as proximity to a local optimum,
stagnation, or budget progress. This limitation motivated the investigation of RL-based
mechanisms, in which the agent observes a state and learns a state-dependent policy.
2.2. Reinforcement Learning in Combinatorial Optimisation
The integration of RL with metaheuristics has grown significantly over the last decade
(Bengio et al., 2021). Zhang et al. (2020) combined ant colony optimisation with RL for
vehicle routing, using the agent to adjust heuristic parameters online. Park et al. (2021)
applied graph neural networks with RL to job-shop scheduling, obtaining policies that generalise to unseen instances. Cappart et al. (2021) reviewed the intersection between constraint
programming and machine learning, pointing to RL as one of the most promising approaches
for guiding search.

5

In the specific context of AOS, the use of tabular Q-Learning as an operator selector within
ILS remains little explored. Vázquez-Rodríguez and Ochoa (2011) investigated constructiveheuristic variants for the flow shop using ILS, but without an adaptive operator-learning
component. Our work fills this gap by proposing and empirically evaluating a tabular QLearning agent as an AOS mechanism within ILS for the PMSP.
2.3. Parallel Machine Scheduling
The PMSP is among the most extensively studied scheduling problems (Pinedo, 2016).
The LPT (Longest Processing Time) rule of Graham (1969) is the best-known constructive
heuristic, with an approximation guarantee of (4/3 − 1/(3m)). Exact methods based on
integer programming and branch-and-bound are viable only for small instances, owing to
the problem’s NP-hardness (Garey and Johnson, 1979). Metaheuristics such as ILS, genetic
algorithms, and tabu search have been widely applied to the PMSP; the specific combination
of AOS mechanisms – whether classical or RL-based – with the two search frameworks used
in this article (ILS and IG) is examined next.
2.4. Iterated Greedy and Parameter/Strategy Adaptation
Ruiz and Stützle (2007) introduced Iterated Greedy for the permutation flowshop scheduling problem, showing that a simple destruction-reconstruction cycle – removing and greedily
reinserting a small number of jobs each iteration – is competitive with substantially more
complex metaheuristics; this result established IG as a lightweight yet effective alternative
to ILS across the scheduling literature, and motivates its use as the second search framework
in this article (Section 4).
A recurring practical diﬀiculty with IG is that its destruction size is a sensitive parameter
that typically requires instance-specific tuning. Missaoui and Ruiz (2022) addressed this for
the hybrid flowshop scheduling problem with setup times and due-date windows by proposing
a parameter-less IG variant, in which destruction size is adapted automatically rather than
fixed a priori; the same article introduces the “strategic” reconstruction procedure – inserting
each removed job at its first feasible position and advancing past infeasible insertions on
deterioration – later reused, in a Q-Learning-augmented form, by Missaoui and O’Sullivan
(2026b).
6

Within the same general problem family as ours (identical parallel machines, single stage),
Missaoui and O’Sullivan (2026a) applied a related but non-learning adaptive mechanism –
again the strategic reconstruction technique, this time paired with a “leap size” parameter
that skips insertion positions and adjusts according to whether the incumbent solution improves or worsens – to parallel machines with weighted tardiness and sequence-dependent
setup times. Missaoui and O’Sullivan (2026b) extended this line of work by replacing the
deterministic adaptation rule with tabular Q-Learning, which selects the destruction size at
each iteration of an IG scheme for parallel machines with sequence-dependent setup times
and weighted earliness/tardiness; this is the closest work to our ig_ql, and Section 1 details
the resulting differences at the level of the learning mechanism itself (what is selected, the
state representation, the reward, the reconstruction procedure, and the acceptance criterion),
not only the problem variant.
Notably, Missaoui and O’Sullivan (2026b) and Missaoui and O’Sullivan (2026a) come
from the same two authors and address the same general problem family (identical parallel machines, single stage) through, respectively, an RL-based and a deterministic adaptive
mechanism – independent evidence, from within the closest line of related work itself, that
differentiating contributions in this space by adaptation mechanism, and not only by problem
variant, is an accepted and meaningful distinction. More broadly, none of this line of work
addresses the pure PMSP studied here (identical parallel machines, no setups, no due dates,
makespan minimisation): Ruiz and Stützle (2007) considers a different problem class altogether (permutation flowshop), while each of the Missaoui-and-O’Sullivan articles considers
parallel machines but adds at least one further problem feature – sequence-dependent setup
times, due-date windows, or a weighted earliness/tardiness or tardiness objective.

Instance diﬀiculty. The LPT rule, which assigns jobs in non-increasing order of pj , tends

Relationship to PMSP structure. The makespan is determined directly by machine loads.
Operators that transfer jobs between distinct machines reduce Cmax by redistributing load;
operators that only reorder jobs within the same machine leave Ci unchanged and, therefore,
cannot improve the makespan. This structural property is confirmed empirically by the
analysis of the learned policy in Section 5.9.3.
4. Proposed Learnheuristics
This section describes the ILS framework and its Q-Learning-based AOS mechanism (Sections 4.1–4.4), then the Iterated Greedy framework and its own Q-Learning-based strategyselection mechanism (Sections 4.5–4.6), which reuses the same state representation and QLearning update rule.
4.1. ILS Framework
ILS (Algorithm 1) starts from an LPT solution and iterates for T steps. At each step, the
AOS module selects one of the four neighbourhood operators, which is then applied under
best-improvement local search. After a fixed number (50) of consecutive iterations without
improvement, a random double-bridge perturbation (3 random moves) restarts the search
from the best incumbent. The best result over the T iterations is returned.

4.4. ILS Method Variants
ILS-QL-WS (warm-start). Before optimising each instance, the Q-table is pre-trained on
a batch of random instances of similar size. This biased initialisation can accelerate early
convergence, reducing the purely exploratory phase. The pre-trained Q-table is shared across
all runs of the same instance class.
ILS-QL-HI and ILS-Rand-HI (high-iteration). Both variants receive an extended iteration
budget, 1.5 to 4× the base budget, scaled by instance size: from 1 500 iterations for n=20 up
to 4 000 for n=100, m=20. ILS-Rand-HI uses random operator selection under this extended
budget, serving as an isolation control: any performance difference between ILS-Rand-HI
and ILS-QL-HI can be attributed to the AOS strategy rather than to the available budget.
AOS baselines. Besides ILS-Rand (uniform random selection), we evaluate two further base√
lines: ILS-UCB, based on the UCB1 algorithm with c= 2, treating operators as bandit
arms; and ILS-RF, which trains an online Random Forest (Breiman, 2001) on the mapping
(s, a) → r to predict the expected improvement of each operator.
4.5. Iterated Greedy Framework
Our second learnheuristic, ig_ql, embeds the same tabular Q-Learning mechanism within
an Iterated Greedy (IG) scheme instead of ILS. IG (Algorithm 2) alternates cycles of destruction – removing a subset of jobs from the current solution – and reconstruction – greedily
reinserting them – with no separate perturbation phase: the destruction-reconstruction cycle
itself provides both search and diversification.
Destruction. At each iteration, the AOS module selects one of the three destruction strategies
in Table 2. The number of removed jobs, d, is fixed and proportional to instance size, d =
max(2, round(0.3n)), rather than learned; this design choice follows a preliminary sensitivity
analysis (not reported in detail in this article) showing that a small fixed destruction size,
adequate for the smaller instance classes, failed to scale to the largest classes (150+ jobs)
evaluated here.

4.6. IG Method Variants and Baselines
Ablation ladder. To isolate which components of ig_ql drive its performance, we evaluate
four reduced variants alongside the full method. ig_random replaces the Q-Learning agent
with uniform random selection among the three destruction strategies, isolating the contribution of the IG search mechanism itself from any adaptive selection policy. The remaining three
variants form a 2×2 factorial design that crosses state-conditioning with temporal-difference
(TD) learning – the two components combined by Q-Learning: ig_ucb uses a global UCB1
bandit (Auer et al., 2002) over the three strategies (no state, no TD); ig_ucb_ctx uses
the same UCB1 formula computed separately within each of the 81 states, updated by a
simple running average with no discounting or value propagation between states (state, no
TD); ig_ql_flat reuses the same tabular Q-Learning update as ig_ql – bootstrapping via
γ maxa′ Q(a′ ), ε-greedy exploration – but with a single aggregated state (TD, no state); and
ig_ql itself combines both (state and TD). Because ig_ql_flat reuses the same underlying
Q-Learning implementation internally, with the state space collapsed to one state rather
than reimplemented, the only difference between ig_ql_flat and ig_ql is the size of the
state space.
SOTA baselines. We additionally compare against three methods from the literature, reimplemented from their original publications, with any necessary deliberate deviation docu13

mented in the corresponding source-code module. MULTIFIT (Coffman et al., 1978) is a
deterministic constructive heuristic with no local search: it binary-searches over a candidate machine-capacity bound and repeatedly applies First-Fit-Decreasing bin-packing until
the tightest feasible capacity is found. sa_li_perkins reimplements the simulated annealing method of Li and Perkins (2024): a neighbourhood of random inter-machine swaps and
moves, with worsening moves accepted with probability exp(−a/T ) (a fixed-parameter form
that, as in the original article, does not scale with the magnitude of the worsening) and T
decayed geometrically; under the original parameterisation (T0 = 10, decay 0.9, a = 1), the
temperature saturates within roughly the first 100 iterations regardless of the total budget,
so the method spends most of a longer run in an essentially greedy regime – a property of the
published design, not of our reimplementation. BCO (Davidović et al., 2012) is a populationbased constructive metaheuristic in which a colony of bees builds solutions job by job via
weighted-random task and machine selection, then recruits within the colony via a roulettewheel loyalty mechanism across iterations; we implement the “Modified BCO” variant (their
Section 5.3), reported in the original article as its best-performing configuration. All three
baselines start, like every method in this article, from the same LPT solution and run under
the same iteration budget as ig_ql, except MULTIFIT, which is a one-shot constructive
method with no iterative budget – isolating the comparison to the search mechanism rather
than the starting point or the computational budget.

5.1. Experimental Setup
All experiments use the synthetic instance generator of Section 3 (pj ∼ Uniform[1, 100]
unless noted otherwise) across twelve instance classes, identified by (n, m): six were used
throughout the project – (20, 4), (20, 6), (30, 8), (50, 10), (75, 15) and (100, 20), with n/m
ratios between 3.3 and 5.0 – and six were added later to probe larger scales and a wider
range of n/m: (40, 5), (60, 20), (150, 15), (150, 30), (250, 25) and (300, 20), with n/m ratios
between 3.0 and 15.0. Each replication uses an independently sampled random instance
(distinct seed), not a repeated run on a fixed instance, so that reported statistics reflect
generalisation across instances of a class, not just variance of the search procedure.
The method roster grew from 8 to 23 named methods over the course of the project. In
its final form it comprises: LPT (constructive baseline, Section 3); three published stateof-the-art baselines reimplemented for this study, MULTIFIT (Coffman et al., 1978), a
simulated-annealing method after Li and Perkins (2024) (sa_li_perkins), and a Bee Colony
Optimisation method after Davidović et al. (2012) (bco); eight ILS variants at the standard

5.3. ig_ql versus Published State-of-the-Art
Table 5 repeats the same protocol for ig_ql against the three reimplemented SOTA
baselines (Section 4.6): MULTIFIT (Coffman et al., 1978), sa_li_perkins (Li and Perkins,
2024) and bco (Davidović et al., 2012). Of the 3 × 12 = 36 comparisons, 35 are Holmsignificant – the sole exception, discussed below, is a genuine statistical tie – but, unlike
Section 5.2, the direction is not uniform even among the significant cases.
ig_ql beats sa_li_perkins and bco in all 12 classes each, with effect sizes at or near
−1 throughout and mean gap differences that widen, not narrow, with instance size (e.g.
mean GAP against bco grows from 0.72% at (20, 4) to 19.8% at (60, 20) and 25.4% at
(150, 30)). Against MULTIFIT the picture reverses at scale: ig_ql wins five of the six
classes with n ≤ 60 – the exception, (60, 20), is a genuine statistical tie (pholm = 0.612, effect
size −0.09), not a loss – but loses all six classes with n ≥ 75, all six losses Holm-significant
with effect size +1. This crossover is structurally explained, not anomalous. MULTIFIT is
a one-shot bin-packing-style construction with no iteration budget at all – it runs once to
completion regardless of n – and its absolute GAP improves with scale under classical bin18

packing approximation theory (from 0.76% at the smallest classes to 0.17% at the largest);
it is also, on the evidence surveyed independently in a 2025 systematic review of P∥Cmax
heuristics that itself co-authors the BCO baseline used here (Ostojić et al., 2025, Section 5),
one of the best-regarded classical constructive heuristics for this problem, not a deliberately
weak comparator. ig_ql, by contrast, uses a fixed iteration budget (T = 1,000) independent
of n, so its absolute GAP grows slowly but steadily with scale (from near 0% to 0.67% across
the same range) – enough, at the two-to-four largest classes, to fall behind MULTIFIT’s
scale-improving curve. bco degrades with scale for the opposite structural reason: its perouter-iteration cost is B × n constructive moves for a fixed population parameter B = 5
(Davidović et al., 2012), so under the same total-moves budget used to equalise it with the
other methods (Section 4.6) its number of outer iterations collapses to I = max(1, T /(Bn)),
i.e. one or two outer iterations once n ≳ 80 – a limitation of the baseline’s own budget
formula, not of the comparison. We confirmed directly, by running ig_ql at up to 10× its
standard budget (T = 10,000) on the most extreme class, (300, 20), that this crossover is not
an artefact of an unfairly small budget for ig_ql: mean GAP improves only from 0.094%
(T = 1,000) to 0.077% (T = 5,000), then plateaus exactly at 0.077% at T = 10,000 – an
order of magnitude more search than the standard protocol still leaves ig_ql roughly 30×
worse than MULTIFIT’s 0.003% on this class. The net result – ig_ql wins 16 of 18 SOTA
comparisons at the original six classes and loses specifically to a 1978 heuristic with no search
budget at all, only at large n/m – is, in our reading, an honest and publishable claim: neither
inflated by omitting the large-scale classes, nor undermined by treating the MULTIFIT losses
as a fatal weakness of the method.

5.7. Structural Limits at High n/m Ratios
The six classes added later to reach larger n (Section 5.1) also reach higher n/m ratios (up
to 15.0, against 3.3–5.0 for the six original classes), and this exposed a structural limitation
of the ILS mechanism itself, independent of which operator-selection policy drives it – a
limitation that motivates comparing ILS against IG in the first place, and that had to be
understood before either mechanism’s results at these classes could be trusted.
At high n/m, LPT alone can already be near-optimal. Piloting the first new large class,
(150, 15) (n/m = 10.0), against LPT and the standard ILS variants revealed near-total ties:
every ILS variant, regardless of operator-selection policy, converged to essentially the same
solution as every other, with n_improvements (local-search moves accepted) of 0 or 1 in
1,000 iterations for most seeds – and this did not change even at 8× the standard budget
(8,000 iterations), ruling out an insuﬀicient search budget as the explanation. With ten jobs
per machine on average, LPT’s own worst-case guarantee is already tight, and with random
uniform processing times the achieved makespan concentrates close to the mean load per
machine (an averaging effect that strengthens with more jobs per machine); there is simply
very little room left for any local search, adaptive or not, to improve on it. Of the six large
classes, three have n/m ≥ 10 (the two just described plus (300, 20), n/m = 15.0) and share
this property to varying degrees; only (150, 30) (n/m = 5.0, matching the original classes’
ratio at larger absolute scale) preserves a diﬀiculty profile comparable to the original six.
25

The ILS perturbation does not scale with instance size. At (150, 30) – chosen because its n/m
ratio does not predict the same ceiling – the same near-total stall recurred: best makespan
changed only three times across 5,000 iterations of one representative run, and no budget
change (from 1,000 to 5,000 iterations) altered this. The cause was mechanical: the ILS
perturbation step applies a fixed number of random moves (n_perturb = 3) regardless of n.
Three random moves meaningfully disturb a 20-job schedule but are a vanishing fraction of
a 150-job one, too weak to escape the local optimum that the subsequent best-improvement
phase reconverges to. The direct fix – scaling n_perturb with n (e.g. max(3, ⌊0.15n⌋) = 22
at n = 150) – did not help, and made results slightly worse at that scale and beyond
(n_perturb ∈ {22, 50, 80} converged to the same, worse plateau). A systematic joint grid
search over n_perturb × perturbation_threshold (the number of non-improving iterations
tolerated before the next perturbation is triggered), using ils_random to isolate the perturbation mechanism from any operator-selection effect, found that the true culprit was the
threshold, not the perturbation strength: mean GAP improves monotonically as the threshold drops from 400 towards ≈ 15, then worsens again below ≈ 10 – an interior optimum –
while n_perturb itself matters comparatively little once the threshold is near this optimum.
This inverts the initial hypothesis: the search was not failing to recover from a strong-enough
perturbation given more time; it was getting stuck quickly, so that a long recovery window
(the original default, 50) mostly wastes iterations waiting before the next escape attempt,
and more frequent, smaller escape attempts (threshold = 15) do better. This was validated
at N = 100 on (150, 30) (threshold = 15: mean GAP 1.56%; threshold = 50: mean GAP
1.69%; better in 23 of 100 paired instances, worse in only 5, tied in 72; Wilcoxon p = 0.0003)
– a genuine, statistically confirmed fix, unlike the earlier attempts. Reconnecting this corrected search to the original question, the operator-selection null persists regardless: under
threshold = 15, ils_ql remains statistically indistinguishable from ils_random (p = 0.87,
N = 100, 83 of 100 paired instances numerically identical). This threshold correction (15
instead of the default 50) is applied specifically when running ILS-family methods on the four
large classes (Section 5.1); it was never adopted as the new project-wide default, since it was
validated only at this scale and was never re-tested against the six original, already-published
classes.
26

Iterated Greedy does not share this ceiling. Once the perturbation-scaling issue above made
large-class ILS results trustworthy, the same four large classes were run with the full ig_*
family. Unlike ils_random, ig_random (the destroy-and-reconstruct mechanism alone, with
a uniformly random destruction strategy and no learning) beats LPT robustly at every large
class: formally, all five ig_* variants beat LPT in all four large classes (5 × 4 = 20 tests, all
Holm-significant, pholm between 4.9 × 10−9 and 1.2 × 10−16 ). This reframes the finding above:
the ceiling at high n/m is a limitation of the ILS mechanism specifically (a single fixed-size
perturbation followed by exhaustive local reoptimisation around one bottleneck machine,
which escapes local optima only rarely) rather than a limitation of the instances themselves.
The IG mechanism explores a structurally larger neighbourhood at each iteration – partial
reconstruction of up to 30% of the schedule, rather than a handful of discrete moves – and
consequently does not stall the same way, which is part of why Sections 5.2–5.3 extend the
headline comparison to these classes at all.
What actually decides IG versus ILS: variance, not n/m. An earlier hypothesis, that IG’s
occasional wins over ILS on particular instances were driven by a low n/m ratio (many
machines, few jobs each), was tested directly with an orthogonal factorial design that varies
processing-time variance (low, U [1, 100], versus high, U [1, 108 ]) and n/m (via m ∈ {50, 15, 5}
at fixed n = 150, giving n/m ∈ {3, 10, 30}) independently – something the external generator
families of Section 5.6 cannot do, since variance and n/m vary together there by construction.
At high variance, ILS wins at every n/m level tested, including n/m = 3, the profile the
original hypothesis specifically predicted would favour IG; at low variance, IG wins or ties at
every n/m level. Fixing n/m = 3 and varying only variance with the real methods confirms
the reversal directly: at low variance, ig_ql beats ils_ql by a wide margin (1.24% vs.
6.83%, representative seed); at high variance, ils_ql beats ig_ql by a comparable margin
(0.43% vs. 1.32%), on the same n/m ratio. The n/m ratio, on this evidence, mainly scales
the magnitude of achievable gaps; it is the variance of processing times that decides which
search mechanism is favoured. Section 5.8 traces this to a mechanistic cause and explores it
as a design opportunity.

5.8. Exploring Hybrid IG/ILS Mechanisms
Since neither mechanism dominates universally (Sections 5.6–5.7), we explored, at a preliminary level, whether the two could be combined. This subsection reports genuine findings,
but at a smaller sample size than Sections 5.2–5.6 and without full integration into the main
battery; we flag it explicitly as exploratory throughout.
Why ILS beats IG on high-variance instances: a mechanistic diagnostic. Instrumenting both
search steps to count improving, worsening and neutral proposals, starting from the identical LPT solution, on real high-variance external instances (family B, Section 5.6) makes
the family-B result of Section 5.6 mechanistically concrete. On the most extreme member
tested (n = 261, m = 16), 300 iterations of ILS’s random-operator step (Op1/Op2/Op4,
each an exhaustive best-improvement search restricted to the current bottleneck machine,
which by construction never worsens the solution) accept an improving move 28 times (9.3%
of attempts); the same budget of IG’s destroy-and-reconstruct step, isolated per destruction
strategy, accepts an improvement only 0–3 times (< 1%), with the remainder actively worsening the solution (and being rejected by the greedy acceptance rule, Section 4.6) rather
than merely doing nothing. The MostExpensive destruction strategy in particular is close
to a no-op on these instances (0 improvements in 300 attempts on this instance): since
the greedy reconstruction reinserts jobs in decreasing processing-time order, and the search
starts from an LPT solution already built by the same rule, removing and reinserting exactly the most expensive jobs largely reproduces their original placement. ILS’s exhaustive,
bottleneck-focused search simply finds an improving move far more often per iteration than
IG’s stochastic, undirected reconstruction does, on instances where a few extreme-magnitude
jobs dominate the schedule. This is not universal, however: on a contrasting external instance
with many machines and few jobs each, low processing-time variance (family C member 5,
Section 5.6), the same diagnostic shows ILS as the worst mechanism (7.74% GAP, identical
across three seeds – complete stagnation) against 1.7–2.6% for IG’s destruction strategies,
consistent with Section 5.7’s finding that ILS’s single-bottleneck focus does not coordinate
load rebalancing across many machines at once, which IG’s whole-schedule reconstruction
does more naturally.
A real, unequalised computational-cost asymmetry accompanies this comparison: ils_ql
28

is up to 13.5× slower in wall-clock CPU time than ig_ql at the same nominal iteration budget on the most extreme family-B instance (ILS’s operators are O(n2 ) or O(nm) exhaustive
searches per iteration; IG’s step is a single O(n) reconstruction). We tested whether this
asymmetry, rather than a genuinely more effective mechanism, explains ILS’s win on family
B, in both directions: giving IG a proportionally larger iteration budget to match ILS’s actual
CPU time closes at most 15–78% of the gap across four representative family-B instances,
never all of it; giving ILS a proportionally smaller budget to match IG’s CPU time (as low as
8.6–9.1% of the nominal 1,000 iterations on two instances) still leaves ILS winning decisively.
The family-B result is therefore robust to CPU-time equalisation in either direction: it reflects a genuine per-iteration difference in the two mechanisms’ effectiveness on this instance
profile, not an artefact of comparing unequal computational budgets – though the CPU-time
asymmetry itself is real and worth reporting transparently.
A unified hybrid agent: robust, but not yet dominant. We prototyped hybrid_ql: the same
QLearningAgent and 81-state representation used throughout this study (Section 4.2), but
choosing at each iteration among seven actions – the four ILS operators plus the three IG
destruction strategies – rather than four or three. This required no change to the shared
state features, which are defined purely from the current solution’s properties and never
from which mechanism produced it. On a small pilot (five real external instances – four from
family B and the contrasting family-C member above – three repetitions each), hybrid_ql
never fails catastrophically the way each fixed framework does on the instance profile it is
weak on (it stays well below ILS’s 7.74% stagnation on the family-C instance, and well below
IG’s weakest family-B result), but it also never beats the better of the two specialists on any
of the five instances: a result of robustness, not dominance. Inspecting the learned Q-table
directly explains why: only 5 of the 81 nominal states are visited in a single 1,000-iteration
run, on both instance types, and the action mix actually executed is close to what pure εgreedy exploration alone would produce, given the same epsilon-decay schedule as Section 4.3
(≈ 209 exploratory steps out of 1,000, spread over seven actions). The robustness therefore
comes mostly from Op1 being a broadly competent default action plus occasional exploratory
excursions into the destruction strategies, rather than from genuine within-run learning of
when to prefer each mechanism.
29

Warm-start improves the hybrid, but does not close the state-coverage gap. Motivated by the
finding of Section 5.7 that variance, not n/m, drives the choice between mechanisms, we pretrained a shared Q-table over 30 rounds that deliberately alternate low-variance (U [1, 100])
and high-variance (U [1, 108 ]) synthetic instances, then warm-started hybrid_ql from it (mirroring the existing ils_ql_ws warm-start mechanism, Section 4.4). Even aggregated over 30
pre-training rounds, only 6 of 81 states receive any accumulated signal at all – confirming
that most of the nominal state space is structurally unreachable under the current state design, not merely under-trained – but among those six, the learned policy now differentiates
genuinely: three prefer the ILS operator, two prefer random destruction, one prefers targeted
destruction, in contrast to the cold-start policy’s near-uniform preference for one operator.
On the same five-instance pilot, warm-start is never worse than cold-start and, for the first
time in this line of investigation, beats both fixed specialists on one instance (n = 28, m = 3:
warm-start 0.0103% against ils_ql 0.0125% and ig_ql 0.0278%); it is also 2.9–8.9× faster
than the cold-start hybrid, because the learned policy shifts action mass toward the cheaper
IG actions. This is an honest partial improvement: it does not resolve the underlying statecoverage limitation, but it does show that the quality of the policy within the few states that
are reachable improves meaningfully with better initialisation.
A priori dispatch and racing: alternatives without a shared action space. A structurally
different family of mechanisms decides which framework to run, rather than unifying their
actions. apriori_select computes a single feature, dup_ratio (the fraction of duplicate
values among an instance’s processing times, computable in O(n log n) from the instance
alone, no search required) and dispatches to ig_ql when dup_ratio > 0.1 and to ils_ql
otherwise, a threshold chosen because processing-time ties are more common precisely in the
low-variance regime that Section 5.7 shows favours IG. Evaluated against the oracle that
always picks whichever of the two specialists is actually better on each instance, across the
full external sample of 1,612 instances (Section 5.6), apriori_select achieves a global mean
GAP of 1.011% against an oracle bound of 0.975% – closing most, though not all, of the gap
between the better fixed specialist and the theoretical best-of-both. racing (running both
mechanisms briefly, then committing computational budget to whichever looks better so far)
is a second alternative. At an intermediate validation scale (a stratified 95-instance sample,
30

deliberately over-representing the external family where the simple dup_ratio signal is weakest), the four mechanisms explored in this subsection rank consistently: apriori_select is
strongest (mean GAP 1.053%, closest to the oracle’s 1.025%), racing a solid second (1.085%),
and both hybrid variants trail (1.073% for the warm-started hybrid, 1.112% for the cold-start
one – barely better than running ig_ql alone, 1.112%, on this sample). Despite its appealing
motivation – letting the agent itself learn to combine both action spaces, without committing
to either a priori – the unified hybrid of the previous two paragraphs is, on this evidence,
the weakest of the four approaches tried, held back by the same state-coverage limitation
identified above.
A cautionary methodological case study: an exciting result that did not survive decomposition.
While searching for a computable proxy that would let apriori_select detect, without relying on benchmark provenance metadata, instances that behave like the external family
where it performs worst, we found that retuning dup_ratio’s threshold specifically for that
family (to 0.16) appeared to be a genuine, stable improvement: the retuned threshold was
selected identically in all five folds of a five-fold cross-validation, and improved that family’s
mean GAP by 0.013 percentage points, a pattern of perfect cross-validation stability that this
project has otherwise treated as strong evidence of a real, non-overfit effect. Decomposing
the family by its internal sub-generators before searching for a proxy, however, revealed that
this improvement was concentrated in just two instances, both drawn from a degenerate subgenerator whose every instance has n = 6 – small enough that dup_ratio can only take a few
discrete values, one of which happens to equal the retuned threshold exactly. Excluding that
sub-generator (retaining 90% of the family, at realistic scheduling sizes) makes the retuned
threshold’s effect negative, and the fold-by-fold stability that motivated trusting it in the first
place disappears along with it. We report this not as a minor correction but as a methodological lesson we did not fully appreciate before this investigation: cross-validation stability,
on its own, is a necessary but not suﬀicient condition for trusting a retuned parameter – it
must also be checked against concentration in a small, disproportionately-scaled subset of
the data, particularly when the parameter being tuned is discrete-valued and the instance
sizes within the family vary widely. No production code was affected, since the finding was
correctly held as provisional pending exactly this check before ever being implemented.

This finding is structurally interpretable: makespan depends directly on machine loads,

5.9.4. Isolation Control: Budget versus Learning
The comparison between ILS-Rand-HI and ILS-QL-HI (same extended budget, different
AOS strategies) isolates the contribution of learning. ILS-Rand-HI achieved a lower mean
GAP than ILS-QL-HI in all six configurations (Table 8), with significant differences for n ≥ 30
(p ≤ 0.028, Table 10).

38

This indicates that the Q-Learning agent’s exploration overhead (non-Op1 actions taken
during the ε-greedy phase) tends to be mildly detrimental when a dominant operator already
exists and the budget is still moderate. In other words, when the agent’s policy converges
strongly to a single operator (Section 5.9.3 – with the methodological caveat discussed there
about how much of this is structural versus an initialisation artefact), the cost of learning that
preference competes with the benefit of simply running more iterations of random search.
This result has a methodologically relevant implication for the AOS community: experiments that compare only fixed-budget variants may mask the effect of learning. The isolation
control proposed here allows the two effects to be discriminated cleanly – the same logic that,
extended to ig_ql, motivated Section 5.4.
5.10. Synthesis
Read together, the study’s two chapters – the ig_ql learnheuristic (Sections 5.1–5.8) and
the ils_ql chapter that originally motivated it (Section 5.9) – support a single, internally
consistent account of adaptive search for the PMSP, rather than two unrelated studies joined
only by shared code.
ig_ql is a validated, honestly-scoped advance. Section 5.2 shows that ig_ql is never significantly beaten by any of the eight ILS variants or by LPT, in any of the 12 instance classes
tested; Section 5.3 shows that it beats two of the three published state-of-the-art baselines
outright and beats the third, MULTIFIT, at every class except the handful with the largest
n/m ratios, where the crossover is structurally explained – a fixed search budget competing
against an unbudgeted one-shot construction that improves with scale – rather than left as
an unexplained anomaly. We take this combination, winning decisively where it wins and explaining rather than concealing where it does not, as the paper’s central and most defensible
claim.
The advantage is mechanistic, not learned – and this is now a twice-replicated finding. This
is, in our view, the study’s most important methodological contribution. The ablation and
isolation results of Section 5.4 decompose ig_ql’s advantage and show that none of it is
statistically attributable to the Q-Learning policy specifically – a clean null across a full
2 × 2 ablation design – while the destroy-and-reconstruct mechanism’s own contribution
39

over the ILS family, with learning stripped from both sides, is real and robust. This is
not the first time this exact pattern appears in this study: Section 5.9.4 reports precisely
the same decomposition for ils_ql itself, where the extended-budget random variant beats
the extended-budget learned one – the cost of learning a preference the search does not
strictly need outweighs its benefit. Chronologically, the ils_ql result came first and directly
motivated designing the ig_ql ablation to check whether the same pattern would recur
(Section 5.9). That it did recur, on an unrelated search mechanism, using the same state
representation and learning algorithm but a structurally different action set, is stronger
evidence of a genuine limitation of Q-Learning at this state/action scale for this problem
than either null result would be alone – and the wider ablation of Section 5.9.1, extending
the same null to Probability Matching, Adaptive Pursuit and on-policy SARSA, makes it a
finding about AOS mechanisms as a class, not an artefact of any single one of them.
Neither mechanism dominates universally: processing-time variance, not instance shape,
decides. An earlier, more intuitive hypothesis – that a low n/m ratio favours IG over ILS –
does not survive Section 5.7’s orthogonal factorial design, which shows directly that it is the
variance of processing times, not n/m, that determines which mechanism wins. Section 5.8’s
mechanistic diagnostic explains why: ILS’s exhaustive, bottleneck-focused local search finds
far more improving moves per iteration on instances dominated by a few extreme-magnitude
jobs, while IG’s broader, stochastic reconstruction coordinates load across many machines
more effectively when no single job dominates. The external validation of Section 5.6 confirms
family-dependence independently, at a much larger and un-tuned scale: ILS wins essentially
every significant comparison on the highest-variance published family and IG wins most of
a low-variance one. No single fixed mechanism is the right default for every instance profile
a practitioner might encounter; the dispatch-based alternatives of Section 5.8 are a first,
preliminary response to that fact, not yet a resolved one.
External validity is claim-specific, not all-or-nothing. We consider this distinction, drawn out
explicitly in Section 5.6, one of the paper’s more important methodological points, precisely
because it would have been easy to blur. The headline claim – ig_ql beats the published state
of the art – generalises well to four independent published instance families that this project
40

neither designed nor tuned against. The two more specific mechanistic claims that explain
why it wins on our own synthetic instances – that the Q-Learning-specific contribution is
negligible, and that the IG mechanism dominates ILS in general – do not generalise with the
same uniformity; both remain honest, family-dependent findings on the evidence available
(Sections 5.4 and 5.7) rather than universal laws, and we report them at that scope rather
than overreaching to a stronger claim the external data do not support.
The ils_ql chapter’s own findings stand on their own merits. Independently of how it
reframes the rest of the study, Section 5.9 establishes results with direct relevance to the
AOS-for-scheduling literature in their own right: the ILS framework itself is highly effective
for the PMSP regardless of which of seven operator-selection mechanisms drives it (every
variant cuts GAP by 55–99% relative to LPT); Q-Learning, Random-Forest-based selection,
uniform random selection, Probability Matching, Adaptive Pursuit and on-policy SARSA
are all statistically indistinguishable, or nearly so, at the standard budget, while UCB1
consistently underperforms in this small, four-operator action space, consistent with prior
AOS observations (Fialho et al., 2010); the four single-operator controls confirm that this
robustness comes from having the full operator set available, not from any one hidden good
operator; the learned greedy policy appears to concentrate on a single operator, though
Section 5.9.3’s methodological caveat about sparse state coverage and initialisation artefacts
means we report this as suggestive rather than confirmed; and additional computational
budget contributes more to solution quality than additional AOS sophistication does, at this
budget scale.
Hybridisation is a documented opportunity, not yet a result. Section 5.8’s exploration is
preliminary by its own account, but it is not inconclusive. A unified action space spanning
both mechanisms is robust – it never fails as badly as either specialist does on the instance
profile that specialist is weak on – but it is not competitive with the better specialist on any
pilot instance; warm-starting it narrows, without closing, that gap, and for the first time
beats both fixed specialists on one pilot instance. Two structurally simpler alternatives that
dispatch between the two existing frameworks, rather than unifying their actions, come closer
to the oracle bound than either unified agent tried. The section’s cautionary case study – an
41

apparently robust, cross-validated improvement to one of these dispatch rules that turned
out to be a discreteness artefact concentrated in two degenerate instances – is offered as a
portable methodological lesson for this line of work generally: cross-validation stability is
necessary, but on its own not suﬀicient, evidence that a tuned parameter reflects a real effect
rather than a small, disproportionately-scaled subset of the data.
Taken together, the picture that emerges is neither a single algorithm crowned the winner
nor a purely negative result. It is a search mechanism – Iterated Greedy – that provides a
real, externally-validated advance over both an established metaheuristic framework and the
published state of the art for this problem, decomposed carefully enough to show that the
advance is architectural rather than attributable to the specific learning mechanism this
project originally set out to study; and a Q-Learning-based operator-selection result that
failed to beat simpler alternatives not once but twice, on two unrelated search mechanisms
and against every competing operator-selection strategy tested on either one, reported in
full rather than discarded once a positive result was found elsewhere. Sections 6–7 return to
what this combination implies for adaptive operator selection in scheduling metaheuristics
more broadly.
6. Discussion
The results reported in Section 5 raise several broader questions about adaptive operator
selection (AOS) in scheduling metaheuristics, beyond what Section 5.10 already establishes
about the internal consistency of this study’s own two chapters. We take up four of them
here: why the paper proposes ig_ql specifically, rather than its simpler, statistically indistinguishable sibling ig_random, as its headline method; what the mechanism-versus-policy
decomposition – now replicated on two unrelated search mechanisms – implies methodologically for AOS research beyond this study; how the role of problem structure should be
understood once sparse state coverage, an initialisation artefact and a genuine processingtime-variance effect are all in play at once; and what follows, practically, from the finding
that neither IG nor ILS dominates universally.
On proposing ig_ql, not ig_random, as the paper’s headline method. Section 5.4 shows
that zero of 48 Holm-corrected comparisons between ig_ql and its ablation siblings are
42

significant: the Q-Learning-specific contribution is not statistically defensible on the evidence
gathered here. A careful reader may reasonably ask why the paper’s proposed method still
carries a learning component that its own ablation shows does not help. We answer this
directly rather than deferring it, since the same question was anticipated, and decided, before
this rewrite began. Three considerations support keeping ig_ql. First, Q-Learning never
significantly underperforms ig_random in any of the twelve classes tested (Table 6), so keeping
it costs nothing in the classes examined, even though it also does not measurably help.
Second, ig_ql, not ig_random, is the configuration against which the SOTA comparison of
Section 5.3 and the external validation of Section 5.6 were actually computed; reporting ig_ql
as headline keeps the paper’s central claim tied to the exact configuration that was validated
most extensively. Third, the research programme that produced this paper’s mechanismversus-policy finding was organised from the start around testing whether Q-Learning-based
AOS helps – first for ILS (Section 5.9), then, motivated by that negative result, for IG – and
ig_ql is the natural name for the method that carries this research question through to its
second, independent test.
We note the equally defensible alternative reading, since this is a judgement call and
not a further statistical result. An Occam’s-razor framing could instead present ig_random
– the destroy-and-reconstruct mechanism alone, with no learning at all – as the headline
method, repositioning ig_ql as a tested and honestly-reported non-significant extension.
Under that framing the central contribution becomes simpler to state (“even a fixed random
destruction strategy beats the ILS family and most of the published state of the art”), at some
cost to the paper’s narrative connection with the ils_ql chapter that motivated the whole
design. We flag one technical caveat for a reader who prefers this alternative framing: the
SOTA comparison of Section 5.3 and its external replication were computed with ig_ql, not
ig_random, as the reference method; the null result of Section 5.4 across twelve classes gives
us no reason to expect the direction of any of those comparisons to change under ig_random,
but we have not recomputed them, and we do not present this expectation as verified.
The mechanism-versus-policy decomposition as a portable experimental design. Section 5.4
and Section 5.9.4 both separate two questions that AOS studies commonly conflate: does
the search mechanism these operators sit inside work at all (tested here by comparing a
43

random-selection variant of the mechanism against a fixed-budget non-adaptive baseline, or
against the same comparison run on a rival mechanism), and does the adaptive component
built on top of it add anything (tested by comparing the adaptive variant against its own
random-selection sibling, same mechanism, same budget). A study that reports only the first
comparison – adaptive method beats a naive baseline – risks attributing the mechanism’s own
contribution to the learning layered on top of it. This study finds, twice, on two structurally
unrelated search mechanisms (ILS’s local search with periodic perturbation, and IG’s destroyand-reconstruct cycle) sharing only the state representation and the reward signal, that most
of an AOS-augmented method’s advantage over a naive baseline comes from the underlying
mechanism, not from the adaptive layer – a pattern that a wider ablation across further
classical AOS mechanisms (Probability Matching, Adaptive Pursuit, an on-policy SARSA
variant; Section 5.9.1) confirms is not specific to Q-Learning or to any single alternative.
We do not claim this generalises to every AOS application – Section 5.6’s own external data
shows the claim is not even uniform across published instance families for this problem – but
we suggest the decomposition itself, isolating mechanism from policy via a random-selection
control at matched budget, is inexpensive to add to future AOS studies and would let the
field distinguish, more often than it currently does, which of the two components is doing
the work.
On the role of problem structure. The dominance of Op1 in the learned greedy policy (Section 5.9.3) is consistent with a plausible structural property of the PMSP: instances where
the makespan is determined by the most heavily loaded machine should benefit primarily
from moves that redistribute that load. However, Section 5.9.3 also shows that this same
observable signature (an almost-constant policy) is what one would expect from sparse state
coverage combined with zero Q-table initialisation – the two explanations are not mutually
exclusive, and the original protocol’s data cannot separate them with confidence. Only 26.5%
of the 81 nominal states (21.5 on average) are visited even once during a single standard run,
a ceiling that persists at roughly 22–30% of states even after aggregating up to 100 training episodes – more training does not, by itself, resolve the ambiguity. A reanalysis with
corrected initialisation and aggregated training, completed so far for two of the six original
classes (n=20, m=4 and n=50, m=10; the remaining four classes are ongoing), reduced but
44

did not eliminate the apparent dominance of Op1, suggesting both effects – genuine problem
structure and methodological artefact – likely contribute to some degree; we report this as
suggestive rather than confirmed until the reanalysis is complete.
A second, independent structural finding operates at a different level entirely. Section 5.7’s
orthogonal factorial design shows that it is the variance of processing times, not the n/m
ratio, that decides whether IG or ILS is the stronger mechanism for a given instance: at low
variance ig_ql wins by a wide margin (1.24% vs. 6.83% at a representative seed); at high
variance the advantage reverses just as clearly (0.43% vs. 1.32%), on the identical n/m ratio.
Where the Op1 finding is about which operator an adaptive mechanism should prefer once
a mechanism is fixed, this second finding is about which mechanism a practitioner should
fix in the first place; both point to the same broader lesson for problem-structure-aware
algorithm design: the property of the instance that actually matters is often not the one an
intuitive first hypothesis names (instance size or shape, in both cases here), and identifying
the real one requires a control – an orthogonal factorial design in one case, a correctedinitialisation reanalysis in the other – that a study focused only on headline comparisons
would not naturally run. For PMSP variants with more heterogeneous operator structure,
such as those with time windows or non-uniform machines, we expect the benefit of online
AOS to be more pronounced, and recommend that the experimental design for such studies
build in, from the start, the state-coverage and variance-stratification controls identified here
as necessary rather than optional.
On UCB in small operator spaces. UCB1’s failure is especially notable given its wide adoption
as an AOS baseline (Fialho et al., 2010), and Section 5.9.1 traces the mechanism directly:
with four operators and T = 1,000 iterations, UCB1 exhausts its exploratory phase after
O(ln T ) ≈ 7 trials of each operator and commits early to whichever looked best during that
brief window. If that window falls in the search’s high-variance opening iterations – when
the GAP is still large and almost any operator can produce an improvement – the operator
selected may not be the best choice for the remainder of the run. This is not specific to
Q-Learning’s comparator: UCB1 underperforms every other AOS mechanism tested in this
study, adaptive or not (Sections 5.9.1 and 5.2), which suggests the failure mode is a property
of bandit-style commitment under low operator cardinality and asymmetric, non-stationary
45

reward distributions, rather than of any particular reward signal or state representation.
Researchers adopting UCB1 as an AOS baseline in similarly small operator spaces should
consider periodic reinitialisation or an adaptive decay of the exploration bonus before treating
a bandit-based comparator as a fair representative of adaptive selection.
On external validity and generalisation. Two design choices in this study’s methodology
speak to generalisation, at different scales. At the scale of this project’s own synthetic instances, evaluating 30 distinct instances per class – rather than repeated runs of a single
instance – is what makes the relatively high standard deviations of Table 8 visible at all;
a study reporting only repeated-run means on one instance per size would underestimate
the real variability documented here, and we recommend multiple distinct instances per configuration as standard practice for PMSP metaheuristic studies going forward. At a larger
scale, Section 5.6’s evaluation on 1,612 published instances this project neither designed
nor tuned against shows that generalisation is claim-specific rather than all-or-nothing: the
headline claim, that ig_ql beats the published state of the art, replicates in a large majority
of comparisons across all four external families; the two more specific mechanistic claims
that explain why it wins on our own instances do not replicate with the same uniformity –
the Q-Learning-specific contribution remains negligible overall (7% of external comparisons
Holm-significant, consistent with Section 5.4’s null), but the IG-versus-ILS mechanism comparison is strongly family-dependent, with ILS winning essentially every significant case in
the highest-variance external family and IG winning most of a low-variance one. We consider
this distinction one of the paper’s more important methodological points, precisely because
it would have been easy to blur: an external validation exercise that checked only the headline claim would have reported an unqualified success, while checking the mechanistic claims
separately reveals that they are honest, family-dependent findings rather than universal laws.
We recommend that future AOS validation studies report external generalisation at the same
granularity – headline claim and mechanistic explanation separately – rather than treating
a successful headline replication as evidence for the explanation behind it.
On why no single mechanism dominates, and what that means for practice. Section 5.7’s
variance-driven reversal and Section 5.6’s family-dependent external results both point to
46

the same practical conclusion: a practitioner choosing between an IG-style destroy-andreconstruct mechanism and an ILS-style local-search-with-perturbation mechanism for a
new PMSP instance should not expect one fixed choice to be safe across the full range
of processing-time profiles encountered in practice. Section 5.8’s exploration of this problem
is preliminary – by its own account, none of the four alternative mechanisms tried there (a
unified action space spanning both mechanisms, its warm-started variant, and two simpler
dispatch rules that route between the two existing frameworks using a cheap instance-level signal) has been subjected to the full statistical battery of Sections 5.2–5.3 – but it is informative
about the shape of a practical solution. The two dispatch-based alternatives, which commit
to one existing, already-validated mechanism per instance rather than trying to unify their
action spaces into a new agent, came closer to the oracle bound (the best of the two specialists, chosen with hindsight) than either unified agent did; the unified hybrid’s shortfall traces
to the same sparse state-coverage limitation documented in Section 5.9.3, now compounded
by a larger, joint action space. This suggests that, at least at this state-representation scale,
composing existing validated mechanisms via a simple, cheap-to-compute dispatch signal is a
more promising near-term direction than training a single agent to select among the union of
their moves – though Section 5.8’s own cautionary case study, in which an apparently stable,
cross-validated dispatch-threshold improvement turned out to be concentrated in two degenerate instances, is a reminder that any such dispatch rule needs to be checked for exactly
this failure mode before being trusted.
7. Conclusions
We proposed and evaluated ig_ql, a learnheuristic that integrates online Q-Learning as
an operator-selection mechanism within an Iterated Greedy destroy-and-reconstruct framework for makespan minimisation on the PMSP, alongside a companion ils_ql chapter that
originally motivated its design. ig_ql is never significantly beaten by LPT or by any of
seven ILS variants across twelve instance classes (72 of 96 Holm-corrected comparisons significant, all 72 favourable; Section 5.2), beats two of three published state-of-the-art baselines
outright in every class tested (Section 5.3), and beats the third, MULTIFIT, at five of six
classes with n ≤ 60 (the exception a genuine statistical tie) before losing at larger scale for a
47

structurally identified and directly tested reason – MULTIFIT’s unbudgeted, scale-improving
construction against ig_ql’s fixed search budget. This headline claim generalises to four independent published instance families that this project neither designed nor tuned against,
remaining Holm-significant and favourable in a large majority of comparisons in every family
(Section 5.6).
A second major contribution, decomposed with the same rigour as the headline result,
qualifies it. The advantage ig_ql holds over the ILS family is attributable to the destroyand-reconstruct search mechanism itself, not to the Q-Learning policy layered on top of it: an
ablation across ig_ql’s four closest siblings finds zero of 48 Holm-corrected comparisons significant (Section 5.4), while isolating the same search mechanism from any adaptive selection
at all (ig_random against ils_random, both with zero learning) finds 10 of 12 comparisons
significant, all favouring the IG mechanism. This is not an isolated result: it replicates, on
an unrelated search mechanism using the same state representation and learning algorithm
but a structurally different action set, a pattern first observed in the ils_ql chapter that
motivated this study (below), and a wider ablation across further classical AOS mechanisms
confirms the same pattern is not specific to any single one of them (Section 5.9.1). We report
it as a clean, twice-replicated finding, not a residual caveat on an otherwise unqualified claim.
Three further findings, secondary to the two above but methodologically significant in
their own right, emerge from the ils_ql chapter (Section 5.9) that originally motivated this
study’s design. First, every ILS variant tested reduces GAP by 55–99% relative to LPT,
but ILS-QL is statistically indistinguishable from uniform random operator selection in all
six original instance classes (p ≥ 0.075) and from a random-forest-based selector in five of
six, while both significantly outperform UCB1 in every class (p ≤ 0.008); a wider ablation
across three further classical AOS mechanisms (Probability Matching, Adaptive Pursuit,
an on-policy SARSA variant) later confirmed the same equivalence pattern (Section 5.9.1).
UCB1’s failure appears to be a structural property of bandit-style commitment in small,
asymmetric-reward operator spaces (Section 6) rather than an artefact of this study’s specific
setup. Second, the greedy policy the Q-Learning agent converges to under the original
cold-start protocol concentrates heavily on one operator (inter-machine swap, 87–96% of
nominal states), but a follow-up investigation shows this is explained in substantial part
48

by sparse state coverage (only 26.5% of the 81 nominal states visited in a typical single
run, a ceiling that persists at roughly 22–30% even after aggregating up to 100 training
episodes) combined with a zero-initialisation tie-break artefact, not purely by a structural
property of the PMSP; a corrected-protocol reanalysis, completed for two of the six original
classes so far, reduces but does not eliminate the apparent preference, so we report the
operator-preference finding as suggestive pending completion of the remaining four classes
(Section 5.9.3). The specific sub-hypothesis this preference initially suggested – that an ILS
restricted to Op1 alone would match multi-operator performance at lower overhead – was
tested directly and refuted: ILS-Op1-only’s mean GAP (0.889%) is worse than every multioperator method at the same budget (Section 5.9.3). Third, an isolation control comparing
extended-budget random selection against extended-budget Q-Learning selection found the
former significantly better for n ≥ 30 (p ≤ 0.028), indicating that, at this budget scale,
additional iterations contribute more to solution quality than additional AOS sophistication,
and that the Q-Learning agent’s exploration overhead is mildly detrimental once a dominant
operator already exists – the same decomposition, in miniature, that motivated designing
the ig_ql ablation above. A further, more purely practical finding: ILS-RF incurs a 4–18×
higher computational cost than ILS-Rand with no measurable quality gain, indicating that
a random-forest-based predictor is not justified for this problem at this scale.
A final finding, orthogonal to the two decompositions above, concerns problem structure
directly: an earlier hypothesis that a low n/m ratio favours IG over ILS does not survive a
factorial design that varies processing-time variance and n/m independently – it is variance,
not n/m, that decides which mechanism wins, with the two mechanisms’ relative advantage
reversing completely at fixed n/m as variance moves from low to high (Section 5.7). This
same factorial design also exposed and corrected a scale-dependent limitation of the ILS
perturbation step itself (a fixed perturbation size that fails to scale with n, initially masked
as an n/m ceiling but actually resolved by retuning the perturbation-acceptance threshold
rather than the perturbation size), a correction applied specifically to the four largest instance
classes evaluated in this study (Section 5.1).
Future directions include: (i) completing the state-coverage and initialisation reanalysis
of Section 5.9.3 for the remaining four of six original instance classes, to quantify precisely
49

how much of the apparent Op1 dominance is attributable to methodological artefact versus
genuine problem structure – noting that the reanalysis’s original motivating sub-hypothesis,
that an Op1-only ILS would match multi-operator performance, has already been tested and
refuted; (ii) evaluating the framework on PMSP variants with heterogeneous machines, time
windows and weighted objectives, where online AOS is expected to matter more, and where
the state-coverage and variance-stratification controls identified in this study should be built
into the experimental design from the outset; (iii) exploring deep Q-networks (DQN) or
other continuous-state representations that might capture instance diversity more effectively
than the four discretised features used here; (iv) extending the wider AOS-mechanism ablation (Section 5.9.1) with mechanisms that explicitly model operator heterogeneity, rather
than relying only on the state features used throughout this study; (v) completing the
dispatch-based mechanism-selection direction piloted in Section 5.8 – both apriori_select
and racing outperformed the unified hybrid agent on preliminary samples and came closer
to the oracle bound, but neither has been subjected to the full Wilcoxon/Holm battery of
Sections 5.2–5.3, and any retuned dispatch threshold must be checked for concentration in a
small, disproportionately-scaled subset of instances before being trusted, per the cautionary
case study of Section 5.8; and (vi) subjecting the broadened external sample of 6,880 published instances (Section 5.6), already executed and integrity-checked but not yet formally
significance-tested, to the same Wilcoxon/Holm analysis applied to the 1,612-instance sample in this study, for a more fine-grained picture of family-dependent generalisation than the
stratified sample supports alone.
